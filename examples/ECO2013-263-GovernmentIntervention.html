Government Intervention in Markets

Markets tend to move toward equilibrium in order to eliminate shortages and surpluses. In some cases, governments intervene in the market in order to prevent market forces from working. In this section, we will discuss how governments interfere in markets and what consequences their interference has on suppliers and consumers. Specifically, we will discuss price floors, price ceilings, and taxes.

Governments intervene in markets because of political pressure from buyers and sellers. Buyers always strive to obtain goods at lower prices; if they can make a moral argument that they cannot afford an important good (like housing in New York), they might be able to convince the government to restrict prices and set a price ceiling. Likewise, sellers always strive to sell their goods at higher prices; if they can make an argument that they will go out of business unless they receive the higher price, they might be able to convince the government to set a price floor.

A common theme that we will see throughout this section is that government intervention creates a deadweight loss in the market because it results in the production of an inefficient quantity and thus the loss of surplus. While government intervention may benefit some groups at the expense of others, the net effect of government intervention is a loss of total surplus to society.

Government Intervention: Price Ceilings

A binding price ceiling is a price below the equilibrium price above which it is illegal to charge. The imposition of a price ceiling redistributes surplus between consumers and producers and reduces total economic surplus. The quantity supplied at the price ceiling is less than the quantity demanded, and a shortage results. The quantity transacted will be the quantity supplied because it is the lower of the quantity supplied and the quantity demanded. The quantity transacted under a price ceiling is less than the quantity transacted at equilibrium.

Two practical examples of price ceilings are as follows:

  • Rent controls – Large cities such as New York, San Francisco, and Washington, D.C. often face a situation in which supply and demand produce outrageously high housing prices. The governments of these cities often impose rent ceilings, which dictate the maximum rent that landlords can charge their tenants.

  • Anti-gouging laws – Anti-gouging laws attempt to prevent "price gouging" during times of need by setting the maximum price that can be charged for certain necessities. For example, the Florida government passed price gouging laws establishing maximum price increases for fuel, building materials, and other emergency-related items after Hurricane Ian in 2022.

    At first glance, you might think that anti-gouging laws are good things. After all, we don't want companies jacking up the price of essential things like generators, bottled water, gasoline, and hotel rooms in the aftermath of a storm. The difficulty with this argument is that, like all binding price ceilings, anti-gouging laws create shortages and thus create deadweight losses. This means that some people who want these essential goods and services will not be able to buy them.

  • Price ceilings on other items – In some countries, the government puts price ceilings on basic necessities and other items, especially in times of economic duress. For example, some countries have price ceilings on pharmaceutical drugs. The United States has price ceilings on insulin, and other countries have price ceilings on a wide variety of drugs.

Consider the supply and demand diagram below, which shows the market for textbooks. At the equilibrium price of $100 per unit, the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied are both 500. However, if the government imposes a price ceiling of $75 per unit, the quantity demanded increases to 562.5 and the quantity supplied falls to 375. This leaves a shortage of 187.5 units.

If a price control does not make the equilibrium price illegal, it does not have any impact on the market. Therefore, a price ceiling is only binding, or effective, if it is set below the equilibrium price. In a competitive market, the price will not rise above equilibrium, so any price ceiling set above the equilibrium price will be ineffective. By "ineffective," we mean that the prevailing price in the market will not be determined by the price ceiling; it will be determined by the equilibrium price instead. For example, if the government imposed a $300 ceiling on a bottle of water, it would be ineffective. There's no scenario in which the equilibrium price of a bottle of water would be as high as $300, so the price ceiling simply would not matter.

Excess demand under price ceilings can have several consequences. One of them is the formation of a black market, an illegal market in which goods are transacted at a price that exceeds the ceiling price. For example, suppose that the government imposes a rent ceiling of $500 in a market where average rents are far higher. Sellers might charge buyers a huge upfront fee for the "privilege" of renting the apartment at $500. There are obviously no rent controls in Gainesville, so you have probably not had to deal with the difficulty of finding a rent-controlled apartment. If you lived in New York City or San Francisco where rent ceilings exist, apartment hunting would be much more difficult and time-consuming.

A Price Ceiling's Impact on Producer Surplus, Consumer Surplus, and Total Surplus

A price ceiling has the following effects on the market:

  • It increases the quantity demanded.
  • It reduces the quantity supplied.
  • It reduces the quantity sold.
  • It reduces producer surplus.
  • It reduces total surplus.
  • It usually increases consumer surplus, but consumer surplus could also decrease. (Consumer surplus increases as long as the gain to consumers who get to buy the good at the lower price is greater than the loss to consumers who don't get to buy the good at all because of the ceiling.)

The people who win from a price ceiling are consumers who are able to get the good at the lower price. The groups that lose from a price ceiling are producers who have to sell at a lower price and consumers who don't get to buy the good at the lower price.

Let's graphically examine the impact of a price ceiling in a market. The diagram on the left below shows the distribution of consumer surplus and producer surplus without a price ceiling, and the diagram on the right shows the distribution of consumer surplus and producer surplus with a price ceiling:

Note that, under the $500 price ceiling, the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied by 125 units. Also, note that the quantity transacted under the price ceiling—25 units—is less than the quantity transacted in a competitive market. The darkened triangle to the right of the quantity transacted is the deadweight loss—the efficiency loss that results because the market does not produce the efficient quantity under the price ceiling. In this case, the price ceiling reduced producer surplus, increased consumer surplus, and reduced total surplus.

Price ceilings are typically imposed to increase consumer surplus. Price ceilings do not always increase consumer surplus, but the price ceiling in the previous example did. If this was the goal of the price ceiling in this case, it was successful. However, this desired outcome has come at a cost: the loss of total surplus that we call deadweight loss. (Note that the loss in producer surplus is greater than the gain in consumer surplus, so the "winners" from the policy cannot adequately compensate the "losers.")

Price Ceilings: A Numerical Example

Consider the market for rental apartments. Use the following supply and demand diagram to answer the questions below:

  1. What is the equilibrium price and quantity?

    The equilibrium quantity is the quantity for which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. Total surplus is maximized at this quantity. In the diagram, the equilibrium price is $400 and the equilibrium quantity is 600 units.

  2. What are the consumer surplus and producer surplus at the equilibrium price and quantity?

    The consumer surplus is equal to the area below the demand curve and above the price, to the left of equilibrium. The producer surplus is equal to the area above the supply curve and below the price, to the left of equilibrium. These areas are shown below.

    The relevant areas are calculated as follows:

    \(CS = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Base} \times \text{Height}\)
    \(CS = \frac{1}{2} \times (600) \times (\$900 - \$400) = \$150,000\)

    \(PS = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Base} \times \text{Height}\)
    \(PS = \frac{1}{2} \times (600) \times (\$400 - \$100) = \$90,000\)

    In this case, the consumer surplus was greater than the producer surplus. This does not always have to be the case; it depends on the slope of the demand curve and the slope of the supply curve.

  3. If a price ceiling of $300 per unit is imposed, is there a shortage or a surplus?

    If a price ceiling of $300 is imposed, the quantity demanded will be 720 and the quantity supplied will be 400. The quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied, so there is a shortage of 320 units.

  4. If a price ceiling of $300 per unit is imposed, how many units will be transacted?

    The lower of the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied will determine the quantity transacted. At a price ceiling of $300, 720 units are demanded and 400 units are supplied. Therefore, the quantity transacted will be 400 units.

  5. If a price ceiling of $300 per unit is imposed, what are the consumer surplus and producer surplus?

    The producer surplus is the area below the ceiling price ($300) and above the supply curve, to the left of the quantity transacted (400 units).

    As you can see in the diagram below, this is the area of the PS triangle:

    \(PS = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Base} \times \text{Height}\)
    \(PS = \frac{1}{2} \times (400) \times (\$300 - \$100)\)
    \(PS = \$40,000\)

    The consumer surplus is the area of the trapezoid formed below the demand curve and above the ceiling price, to the left of the quantity transacted. Although you could calculate the area of the trapezoid directly, it is typically easier to break up the area into a triangle and a rectangle and add those areas together.

    As you can see, the producer surplus fell from $90,000 to $40,000 due to the imposition of the price ceiling. The consumer surplus increased from $150,000 to $173,333.33 due to the imposition of the price ceiling.

  6. Did total surplus increase or decrease due to the imposition of the price ceiling?

    The total surplus is the consumer surplus plus the producer surplus. The total surplus before and after the price ceiling is calculated below:

    Before the Price Ceiling:
    \(TS = CS + PS = \$150,000 + \$90,000 = \$240,000\)

    After the Price Ceiling:
    \(TS = CS + PS = \$173,333.33 + \$40,000 = \$213,333.33\)

    As you can see, the total surplus decreased by $26,666.67 due to the imposition of the price ceiling. This lost surplus is called the deadweight loss, and it can be calculated as the area to the right of the quantity transacted and to the left of the equilibrium quantity, below the demand curve and above the supply curve:

    \(DWL = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Base} \times \text{Height}\)
    \(DWL = \frac{1}{2} \times (600 - 400) \times (\$566.67 - \$300)\)
    \(DWL = \frac{1}{2} \times (200) \times (\$266.67) = \$26,666.67\)

    The deadweight loss can be thought of as the amount by which total surplus falls when the market is not in equilibrium, or as the total surplus associated with units that do not get produced. The existence of a deadweight loss in this market illustrates that the price control has made the market inefficient.

Another Example: Price Gouging Laws

A price gouging law is simply a government-imposed price ceiling on either prices in general, or on certain goods, such as goods related to disaster relief. For example, suppose that a hurricane hits Florida, leaving many without power. This would naturally increase the demand for generators. Without any government-imposed price ceilings, the impact on the market for generators would be shown in the diagram below. The equilibrium price of a generator would increase from $600 to $800, and the equilibrium quantity would increase from 1,000 to 1,200.

However, if the government were to impose a $600 price ceiling on generators, the quantity of generators demanded at $600 (1,400) would be greater than the quantity of generators supplied at $600 (1,000). Therefore, a shortage of 400 generators would result. The people who are able to buy a generator at the lower price will benefit, but two groups will lose from this price control: producers who are forced to accept a price less than equilibrium and consumers who are unable to buy the generators they want at the price they're willing to pay.

Government Intervention: Price Floors

A binding price floor is a price above the equilibrium price below which it is illegal to charge. The imposition of a price floor redistributes surplus between producers and consumers and reduces total economic surplus. The quantity supplied at the price floor is greater than the quantity demanded, and a surplus results. The lower of the quantity demanded and quantity supplied determines the quantity transacted, so the quantity demanded will be the quantity transacted. The quantity transacted under a price floor is less than the quantity transacted at equilibrium.

Two real-world examples of price floors are as follows:

  • The minimum wage – The minimum wage is the minimum hourly rate that employers can pay their employees. We will discuss the minimum wage in greater detail later in these notes.
  • Agricultural price controls – The government sometimes dictates the minimum amount that may be paid for certain agricultural goods. These price controls are in place to guarantee domestic farmers a minimum amount of profit so that they remain viable.

A price floor set below the equilibrium price will not be binding (that is, it won't have any effect on the market) because the equilibrium price—and not the price floor—will determine the quantity transacted. For example, if the government mandated that new cars could not be sold for less than $50, the price floor would not have any impact. Likewise, if the New York City government said that the minimum wage rate for workers in New York City were 25 cents per hour, it would have no effect on the labor market in New York City.

The diagram below compares a market with no price floor to a market in which a binding price floor has been established:

At a price floor of $750, the quantity demanded is 75 units, the quantity supplied is 125 units, and there is a surplus of 50 units. The quantity transacted is 75 units—25 units fewer than the quantity transacted in a market with no price floor.

As you can see, an effective price floor typically has the following effects in the market:

  • It reduces the quantity demanded.
  • It increases the quantity supplied.
  • It reduces the total quantity sold.
  • It reduces consumer surplus.
  • It reduces total surplus.
  • It usually increases producer surplus, but producer surplus could also decrease. (Producer surplus increases as long as the gain to producers who get to sell the good at the higher price is greater than the loss to producers who don't get to sell the good at all because of the floor.)

A Specific Example of a Price Floor: The Minimum Wage

Price floors can also be found in the labor market in the form of a minimum wage—a wage below which it is illegal to pay workers. The market for labor is similar to the market for any good or service, but the roles of firms and individuals are reversed. In most markets, the individuals are the demanders and the firms are the suppliers. In the market for labor, however, firms are the demanders and individuals are the suppliers. Like any market, the market for labor is made up of an upward-sloping labor supply curve and a downward-sloping labor demand curve. The price of labor is the wage rate.

The minimum wage is the most prominent example of a price floor. The federal minimum wage is currently $7.25 per hour, though most states have a higher state minimum wage. Many people believe that the minimum wage should be much higher, and there has recently been a movement to increase the federal minimum wage to $15 or $20 per hour. A number of states (including Florida) have recently increased their minimum wages.

Consider the following example, which illustrates a minimum wage acting as a binding price floor in the labor market: Suppose that the equilibrium wage is $15 per hour and the minimum wage is set at $20 per hour. The market diagram might look like this:

As you can see, a binding minimum wage creates a situation in which the quantity of labor supplied exceeds the quantity of labor demanded. We call this unemployment. Note that the quantity of labor supplied increases due to the imposition of the minimum wage. This is one reason why the unemployment rate increases when a minimum wage is imposed. That is, not all of the workers who are unemployed at the minimum wage would be willing to work at the equilibrium wage.

Note this subtle point: When a minimum wage is imposed, the amount of unemployment increases by more than the amount that employment decreases. Employment decreases from the original equilibrium quantity to the new quantity of labor demanded. However, the amount of unemployment increases by even more because more people are willing to work at the higher wage rate. That is, unemployment increases for two reasons: the quantity of labor supplied increases (because people are more willing to work at a higher wage rate) and the quantity of labor demanded decreases (because firms are less willing to hire workers at a higher wage rate).

In the diagram above, total employment decreased by 100 hours (from 300 hours to 200 hours) as a result of the minimum wage. However, unemployment increased by 200 hours (the difference between the 400 hours supplied and the 200 hours demanded). This is because the quantity of labor supplied increased when the wage rate increased.

As you can see, the consumers of labor (i.e., businesses) are made worse off. Some producers of labor (i.e., workers) are made better off because they receive a higher wage. However, some producers of labor (i.e., workers) are made worse off because they no longer have a job. On the whole, the minimum wage helps workers as a group, but it reduces society's total surplus because the harm to businesses and individuals who no longer have a job is greater than the benefit to workers overall.

More on the Minimum Wage

The group that wins from a binding minimum wage is those less-skilled workers who keep their jobs and enjoy the higher wage rate. The groups that lose from a binding minimum wage are workers who become unemployed as a result of the new minimum wage and firms that have to pay a higher price for labor.

The economic theory predicts that an increase in the minimum wage should cause an increase in unemployment. However, economists have historically not seen this in the data. Two commonly cited explanations are the following:

  • The quantity of labor demanded and the quantity of labor supplied are not very responsive to small changes in the wage rate. Empirical studies show that the demand for labor and supply of labor are both relatively inelastic (i.e., the labor demand and labor supply curves are steep) at low wage rates, and over small intervals. This suggests that small increases in the minimum wage may not result in noticeable increases in unemployment.

    In fact, in the early 1990s, labor economists collected data that illustrated that small-scale increases in the minimum wage have no impact on unemployment. In these studies, researchers studied the Philadelphia labor market. The Philadelphia metro area spans over three states—New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware. New Jersey increased its minimum wage, while Pennsylvania didn't, so researchers expected to find that unemployment increased in New Jersey but not in Pennsylvania. However, they found no differential impact in the two states. Since then, several other studies have had similar results. Advocates of increases in the minimum wage point to this research as an argument in favor of increasing the minimum wage.

  • Historically, most increases in the minimum wage have been small. In fact, many states have not even increased their minimum wage enough to keep up with inflation. This makes it difficult to detect any resulting increase in unemployment because the supply and demand for low-skilled labor are both very inelastic (meaning the supply and demand curves are vertical). Looking for the impact of small-scale increases in the minimum wage on the unemployment rate is therefore like looking for a statistical "needle in a haystack."

    Until recently, there had been no large-scale experiments analyzing large increases in the minimum wage. However, there have recently been movements for significant increases in the minimum wage. Soon, economists will have enough data to determine whether large increases in the minimum wage do have a significant impact on unemployment. Some economists believe that these studies will likely reveal that large-scale increases in the minimum wage do increase unemployment, but more research needs to be done to determine whether they're right.

Does this mean that the government should not raise the minimum wage? That's up for you to decide. There are other ethical and non-economic considerations as well. For example, some people believe that the government should absolutely implement policies that increase the incomes of certain members of society. Other people believe that these redistributive policies are unethical and should not be pursued.

If the goal of the minimum wage is to eliminate poverty, it may not be the best policy alternative for two reasons:

  • The poorest people in society have no jobs at all, so the minimum wage does not affect them.
  • Many people who earn the minimum wage are high school and college students who are not poor; they're just working to earn some extra spending money.

Another way to think of this is as follows: If the minimum wage is like medicine used to combat poverty, we're not treating the sickest people—we're giving it to people who aren't sick at all.

Government Intervention: Taxes

The government collects taxes in order to raise revenues to fund government spending. In this section, we will examine the impact of taxes without considering the value of the spending programs that taxes fund. We will see that taxes reduce the quantity transacted and create a deadweight loss, but the benefit of the spending programs funded by the taxes may outweigh this loss. We will revisit this later in the semester when we discuss fiscal policy.

We will begin our discussion of taxes by distinguishing between quantity taxes and ad valorem taxes. We will then show the economic impact of a quantity tax.

Types of Taxes: Quantity Taxes vs. Ad Valorem Taxes

Governments use a number of different kinds of taxes. We will focus on an excise tax (also called a quantity tax), which is a per-unit quantity tax that is imposed on every unit sold. Another type of tax is an ad valorem tax, such as a sales tax, which is typically set as a percentage of the sales price. Property taxes are other examples of ad valorem taxes.

Many excise taxes are on goods that the government wants to dissuade people from using, like cigarettes and alcohol. For example, the government might impose a per-unit tax on every pack of cigarettes, every liter of alcohol, or every gallon of gasoline sold. These taxes are sometimes called "sin taxes."

To illustrate the difference between a quantity tax and an ad valorem tax, suppose you buy a new sweater at Uniqlo or H&M. A quantity (excise) tax would be a fixed amount per unit—for example, $20 per sweater—so you'd pay the same tax whether the sweater costs $400 or $200. An ad valorem tax, by contrast, is a percentage of the purchase price—for example, 10%—so you would pay more tax on the more expensive sweater.

Here's a test that you can use to determine whether a tax is a quantity tax or an ad valorem tax: If the tax is calculated as a percentage of the price, then tax revenue per unit rises when the price rises (ad valorem tax). If the tax is a fixed dollar amount per unit, then the tax per unit does not change when the price changes (quantity tax).

Only a handful of taxes, such as taxes on alcohol and cigarettes, are quantity taxes in the "real world," but the economic analysis of a quantity tax is easier than the economic analysis of any other kind of tax. The intuition that we gain from analyzing the impact of a quantity tax can be generally applied to other types of taxes.

Example: The Gasoline Tax

The gasoline tax is an example of an excise tax because it is a standard amount for every gallon of gasoline. This tax has not changed much for several decades, which is problematic because it is used to fund the interstate highway system. As cars have become more fuel-efficient (and some are electric), consumers are now driving more and using less gasoline. This means that the interstate highway system is being funded by a shrinking pool of revenue. Some have called for an increase in the gasoline tax, but this is a contentious and highly political issue.

The Economic Impact of a Quantity Tax Paid by the Seller

Let's consider a quantity tax that must be paid by the seller. Specifically, let's assume that the government imposes a $3 per unit tax on sushi rolls—a tax that must be sent to the government by the sellers. Such a tax would increase the minimum amount that sellers must receive for each unit by $3 and would therefore cause an upward shift of the supply curve by $3. Note that this is a vertical shift of the supply curve because the tax essentially increases the amount that sellers must receive for each unit by the amount of the tax. The change in producer surplus and consumer surplus is shown below:

As you can see, the imposition of the $3 tax on suppliers shifted the supply curve up by $3. The price that consumers have to pay increased by $1 (from $15 to $16), and the price that producers receive and get to keep fell by $2 (from $15 to $13). In this case, producers bear a greater share of the tax burden than consumers, but both consumer surplus and producer surplus fell as a result of the tax.

Note that the government generated $72 in tax revenue from this tax: $3 per unit on a total of 24 units transacted under the tax. The number of units transacted under the tax was 6 units fewer than the number of units that would have been transacted without the tax. The deadweight loss ($9) is the area of lost surplus because those units were not transacted.

If the tax were imposed on the consumers rather than on the sellers, we would show a downward shift of the demand curve rather than an upward shift of the supply curve. However, every other economic impact would be the same: The quantity transacted would fall, consumers would pay more, sellers would keep less, the government would generate tax revenue, consumer surplus and producer surplus would fall, and a deadweight loss would be created. All of these qualitative results would also be the same if we were considering an ad valorem tax rather than an excise tax, though the quantitative results (i.e., how much these values change) would be different.
