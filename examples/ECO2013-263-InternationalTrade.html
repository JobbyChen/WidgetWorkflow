Introduction to Global Markets and International Trade

We have already discussed international trade to some extent earlier in the semester when we discussed production possibilities frontiers, absolute advantage, and comparative advantage. Remember that the theory of comparative advantage states that countries can gain from trade by specializing in the production of the good for which they have a comparative advantage and trading with one another. A country has a comparative advantage in producing a good if it can produce that good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.

We will now look at the impact of international trade on individual markets, such as the market for automobiles. In this section, we will briefly discuss who wins and loses from international trade, and we will attempt to put our economic analysis into a supply and demand framework.

Note that the basics are the same as the theory of comparative advantage: a nation will be a net exporter of a good if its autarky (no-trade) domestic price is lower than the world price, and it will be a net importer of a good if its autarky domestic price is higher than the world price. If we assume that demand conditions are the same everywhere—an assumption that does not always hold true—a country will tend to have a lower autarky price for a good when it has a comparative advantage in producing that good. In the supply-and-demand framework, a country will export a good when its autarky (no-trade) domestic price is below the world price, and it will import when its autarky domestic price is above the world price. Comparative advantage helps explain why those price differences exist.

Some Definitions

Imports are goods and services that are produced abroad and purchased by domestic consumers, firms, or governments, while exports are goods and services that are produced domestically and purchased by foreign consumers, firms, or governments. For example, if a Tesla is produced in the United States and purchased by a family in Mexico, that purchase is a U.S. export (and a Mexican import).

Exports are defined broadly — it's anything produced in the United States that is consumed by a foreign consumer, even if it never leaves the country. What matters is where the good or service is produced and who consumes it, not where it is consumed. For example, if a German family vacations in Miami and goes out for Cuban food, that meal is still counted as a U.S. export because it was produced in the U.S. but consumed by foreign visitors (and, from Germany’s perspective, it is an import). Along the same lines, tourism and other services sold to international visitors count as exports.

A car produced at Tesla's Texas plant would NOT count as an import if it's bought by a U.S. household because it was produced in the United States.

Another example of a "non-shipped" export is education. If an international student at UF pays tuition and takes classes in Gainesville, those educational services are treated as a U.S. export, because the service is produced here but purchased by someone from abroad. By contrast, if someone is living in the U.S. as a resident (not temporarily here as a visitor), their purchases are generally not treated as exports. Trade does not have to involve physical goods, either. If you hire an accountant overseas to prepare your tax return and exchange the documents by email, that tax return is an imported service.

The United States is a net importer, meaning that the value of our imports exceeds the value of our exports. However, we are a net exporter of some goods and services—especially services.

In this section, we will use a supply and demand framework to explain who gains and loses from trade. We will assume that we are dealing with perfectly competitive markets. Specifically, we will make the following assumptions:

  • Prices are determined by supply and demand, and goods are identical worldwide.
  • There are two relevant prices: the world price that is determined by the supply and demand for the entire world, and the domestic price that is determined by the supply and demand for the country in question. Domestic consumers could purchase as much of the good or service as they want at the world price, and domestic producers could sell as much of the good or service as they want at the world price. The world price is simply the equilibrium price in the international marketplace, determined by global supply and global demand—it is not a price floor or price ceiling, because it is not set by the government.
  • Countries that open up their doors to trade are price-takers. This means that they can buy and sell all they want at the world price, but they are not large enough to have any significant impact on the world price.

How to Determine Whether a Country Is a Net Importer or Exporter

When a nation opens its borders to trade, we can tell whether it will be a net importer or a net exporter of a good by comparing the world price with the domestic price.

  • When the domestic price is greater than the world price, the nation will be a net importer of the good. The quantity that the country will import is equal to the difference between the domestic quantity demanded and the domestic quantity supplied at the world price.
  • When the domestic price is less than the world price, the nation will be a net exporter of the good. The quantity that the country will export is equal to the difference between the domestic quantity supplied and the domestic quantity demanded at the world price.

Of course, if the domestic price equals the world price, the nation will be neither a net importer nor a net exporter of the good.

Focus on Exports

Consider a case in which the domestic price without trade is less than the world price. As the discussion above indicates, the nation in this case would be a net exporter of the good to the rest of the world. For example, the United States might export financial services to the rest of the world if it can provide those services at a lower price than the rest of the world.

Remember that we assumed that a country can sell as many units as it wants at the world price. Allowing trade therefore drives the domestic price up to the world price, and it forces domestic consumers to pay higher prices for their units. Even domestic consumers who buy domestically produced units must pay the world price, because a domestic seller who cannot get the world price at home will simply sell to a foreign buyer who will pay it. At the higher price, the domestic quantity supplied is higher, and the domestic quantity demanded is lower. The difference between the domestic quantity supplied and the domestic quantity demanded is the number of units that the country will export. Unlike a price floor or price ceiling, where the quantity bought and sold had to be the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied, with trade the domestic quantity demanded and quantity supplied no longer have to be equal—the difference is simply exported (or, in the case of imports, imported).

Here is a summary of the impacts of allowing trade in this market:

  • Impact on domestic consumers – Domestic consumers are worse off with trade. Their consumer surplus decreases for two reasons: (1) Fewer consumers get the good, and (2) consumers are forced to buy the good at the higher world price.
  • Impact on domestic producers – Domestic producers are better off with trade. Their producer surplus increases for two reasons: (1) They sell more units, and (2) they can charge the higher world price.
  • Impact on society as a whole – Society as a whole is better off with trade. The gain in producer surplus exceeds the loss in consumer surplus, so total surplus—the sum of producer and consumer surplus—increases in this market after trade.

Focus on Imports

Consider a case in which the domestic price without trade is greater than the world price. As the discussion above indicates, the nation in this case would be a net importer of the good from the rest of the world. For example, the United States is a net importer of sweaters from the rest of the world. We could certainly make sweaters ourselves, but we import these goods because other countries can make sweaters at a lower price. In this sense, the world price is less than the domestic price.

Remember that we assumed that a country can buy as many units as it wants at the world price. Allowing trade therefore drives the domestic price down to the world price, and it forces domestic producers to accept the lower world price for their units. At the lower price, the domestic quantity supplied is lower and the domestic quantity demanded is higher. The difference between the domestic quantity demanded and the domestic quantity supplied is the number of units that the country will import.

Here is a summary of the impacts of allowing trade in this market:

  • Impact on domestic consumers – Domestic consumers are better off with trade. Their consumer surplus increases for two reasons: (1) More consumers get the good, and (2) consumers are able to buy the good at the lower world price.
  • Impact on domestic producers – Domestic producers are worse off with trade. Their producer surplus decreases for two reasons: (1) They sell fewer units, and (2) they have to accept the lower world price.
  • Impact on society as a whole – Society as a whole is better off with trade. The gain in consumer surplus exceeds the loss in producer surplus, so total surplus—the sum of producer and consumer surplus—increases in this market after trade.

The difference between the two—40 units—is the amount that the country imported. Allowing trade in this market increased consumer surplus and reduced producer surplus. The increase in consumer surplus exceeded the decrease in producer surplus, so there was a net increase in total surplus. The $20 increase in total surplus is the gain from trade.

Note that the domestic production of avocados went from 40 units to 20 units. This means that there are fewer jobs in the U.S. avocado industry. However, this does not necessarily mean a reduction in employment overall. Firstly, avocado makers can eventually transition into jobs in other industries that benefit from trade. Secondly, the fact that domestic consumers can buy avocados at a lower price from the rest of the world means that they have more money to spend on other goods and services. This boosts employment in other domestic industries. In this case, domestic avocado producers lose from trade, but the gain to consumers outweighs their loss.

The Winners and Losers from Trade

The following table shows the effects of allowing trade in an import market and an export market, as compared to a situation in which there is no trade:

Import Market (vs. No Trade) Export Market (vs. No Trade)
Price paid by domestic consumers Lower Higher
Domestic quantity demanded Higher Lower
Domestic quantity supplied Lower Higher
Domestic consumer surplus Higher Lower
Domestic producer surplus Lower Higher
Total domestic surplus Higher Higher

Note that the impacts are all opposite of one another with one key exception: Total surplus is higher after trade whether the country is a net importer or a net exporter. In other words, the winners win more than the losers lose. Unlike price ceilings, price floors, or taxes, opening a market to trade does not create a deadweight loss; instead, it creates gains from trade.

Trade Protection (Tariffs)

As we have discussed, trade has winners and losers. The "losers" often lobby the government for trade protection—a mechanism that restricts trade in an attempt to protect certain constituencies. In this section, we will focus on cases in which the domestic price is higher than the world price and the country is a net importer of the good. When this is the case, free trade harms domestic producers, who often lobby the government for protection.

We will focus on the impact of a specific kind of trade restriction called a tariff, which is a tax on imports. Note that a tariff is only applied to imported goods. For example, suppose that U.S. consumers buy both domestically produced avocados and avocados imported from Mexico. A tariff on avocados would only apply to the imported avocados. Compare this with a sales tax, which would apply to both domestically produced goods and imported goods.

Below, we will see that trade restrictions are harmful to consumers and lead to a net loss in total surplus. As a result, economists tend to oppose them, and instead favor free trade.

We will focus on tariffs in this section, but there are other kinds of trade restrictions and protectionist policies. Examples include quotas (numerical restrictions on the number of imports), tariff-rate quotas (mixtures between tariffs and quotas in which the government allows a certain quantity of imports tariff-free but then applies a tariff to any imports beyond that), and non-tariff barriers (such as government regulations intended to protect domestic industries). These policies all have the same basic welfare effects on consumers, producers, and society in general.

Protectionist policies usually involve restricting imports rather than restricting exports. (Although it is far less common, governments could also tax exports.)

Focus on Import Tariffs

A tariff is a tax that the government imposes on every unit that is imported. The imposition of a tariff effectively increases the world price. This reduces the domestic quantity demanded, increases the domestic quantity supplied, and reduces the amount that the nation imports. In this sense, tariffs do what they are intended to do: they restrict imports and increase domestic production of the good. Here is a summary of the impacts of imposing a tariff in this market:

  • Impact on domestic consumers – Domestic consumers are made worse off by the tariff because they have to pay a higher price for the good, so they consume less of it. As a result, the domestic consumer surplus falls.
  • Impact on domestic producers – Domestic producers are made better off by the tariff because they can charge a higher price for the good, so they sell more of it. As a result, the domestic producer surplus rises.
  • The government – The government is made better off by the tariff because it gains tariff revenue. For example, if the tariff is $0.50 per unit and the quantity of goods imported is 20 units per day, the government gains $10 per day in tariff revenue.
  • Impact on society as a whole – Society as a whole is made worse off by the imposition of the tariff. Specifically, society loses an area of economic surplus equal to the darkened areas in the graph above. These areas are referred to as the deadweight loss that results from the imposition of a tariff.

We will not discuss quotas in detail in this class. Just know that a quota has the same impact as a tariff: It reduces domestic consumer surplus, increases domestic producer surplus, reduces total domestic surplus, and creates a deadweight loss.

If protectionist policies (tariffs and quotas) reduce total surplus, why do governments impose them? Whether to impose a tariff is ultimately a political question rather than an economics question. Elected officials often focus on the increase in domestic production (and the jobs in the protected industry) and pursue the tariff anyway, even though it creates a deadweight loss. In addition, the constituencies that stand to lose from free trade—usually domestic producers in import industries—lobby for trade protection, and producers are generally better able to lobby politicians than consumers.

The "Jobs" Argument Against Trade

A common argument against free trade is that allowing imports hurts domestic businesses and thus causes domestic workers in import industries to lose their jobs. While it is true that allowing imports causes temporary job losses for some workers, economists generally still support free-trade policies because the benefit to domestic consumers exceeds the harm to domestic producers when the nation allows imports.

In fact, economists have shown that the costs of trade protection (in the form of higher prices for domestic consumers) far outweigh the benefits, in terms of saving jobs. Studies of this question have been run on hundreds of industries since the 1980s, and they consistently show that the cost to consumers (in terms of higher prices paid) is many times—usually about 5 to 10 times—the salary earned by a worker whose job is "saved" by protectionist policies. For example, one early study of tariffs on salmon found that consumers paid roughly $500,000 in higher prices to save a single salmon-fishing job that paid about $60,000 a year! For this reason, economists argue that the nation should allow free trade but find other, more efficient ways to help domestic workers. Examples include re-training programs or government subsidies to workers who lose their jobs due to trade.

The cost of anti-trade policies that save jobs in the fishing industry far outweighs the benefit. Nevertheless, these protections remain in place because the fishing industry is effective at lobbying government officials. One reason is that the gains from anti-trade protections are concentrated in a small number of workers, while the costs are spread out over many more consumers.

A tariff does increase production—and therefore employment—in the protected industry, but that is not the same thing as increasing employment overall. Trade certainly negatively impacts jobs in the industries that produce goods that can be imported from the rest of the world at a lower price than the domestic price. However, trade allows consumers to spend less on goods that they import, which increases the demand for other goods and creates jobs elsewhere. Therefore, trade tends to involve job losses in some sectors and job gains in others, and economists generally find that tariffs have no clear effect on overall employment—they tend to move jobs around in the economy rather than create or destroy jobs overall. This is why economists rarely argue that tariffs raise overall employment.

Conclusion

Economists from across the political spectrum generally agree that free trade increases total domestic surplus—there are gains from trade whether a country imports or exports a good—and they generally favor policies aimed at cutting tariffs and other protectionist policies.
