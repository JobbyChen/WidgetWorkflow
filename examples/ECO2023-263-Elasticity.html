Introduction to Elasticity

Elasticity measures responsiveness to change. There are many different elasticity measures, but we will primarily focus on the price elasticity of demand, which is defined as the absolute value of the percent change in the quantity demanded, divided by the percent change in the price.

Example: The Price Elasticity of Demand for Pizza

In the diagrams below, the demand curves have different slopes. Therefore, the same shift in the supply curve causes a different change in quantity demanded and price for both curves.

This phenomenon is known as elasticity. Elasticity is a measure of responsiveness to change. How strongly do people respond to a change in price? Slope is sometimes used to measure elasticity. This is problematic, however, because we have to express the slope in units.

For example, the diagram below shows two demand curves that are the same, except one shows the price in terms of dollars, while the other shows price in terms of cents. The slope for the left graph (rise/run) is −0.5 but the slope for the right graph is −50. It’s obvious that the graphs are expressing the same thing, but because we are expressing the units differently, we cannot use just a number for the elasticity. We have to use units (e.g., $19 per pizza or 1,900¢ per pizza). The same problem exists when expressing the quantities in different ways.

For this course, we will express elasticity as a “pure number.” Elasticity is not expressed in terms of units. We can do this by expressing elasticity as a ratio of percentages.

Numerically, we can define the price elasticity of demand as the absolute value of the percentage change in the quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in the price:

\[E_D = \left| \frac{\%\Delta Q_D}{\%\Delta P} \right|\]

Expressing the elasticity as a percentage allows us to easily compare elasticities across countries without running into different units and money values.

Describing the Price Elasticity of Demand

The price elasticity of demand can range from zero to infinity.

  • Demand is perfectly elastic if ED = ∞. This means that price is fixed; changing the price would completely eliminate any demand. An example of something very close to a perfectly elastic good is one farmer’s milk (and other agricultural goods).
  • Demand is elastic if ED > 1. This means that the quantity demanded is very responsive to a change in price.
  • Demand is unit elastic if ED = 1. This means that a 1% change in price causes a 1% change in the quantity demanded.
  • Demand is inelastic if ED < 1. This means that the quantity demanded is not very responsive to changes in price.
  • Demand is perfectly inelastic if ED = 0. This means that the demand is fixed—a change in price will not change the quantity demanded at all. Examples of goods with demand curves that are close to being perfectly inelastic include life-saving drugs like insulin or drugs used to treat AIDS.

Elasticity Examples

Assume that a 10% price hike leads to a 5% decrease in quantity demanded. What is the price elasticity of demand?

\[E_D = \left| \frac{-5\%}{10\%} \right| = 0.5 \quad \text{(weak response)}\]

Now, assume that a 10% increase in price leads to a 20% decrease in quantity demanded. What is the price elasticity of demand?

\[E_D = \left| \frac{-20\%}{10\%} \right| = 2.0 \quad \text{(strong response)}\]

Now, assume that a 10% increase in price leads to a 10% decrease in quantity demanded. What is the price elasticity of demand?

\[E_D = \left| \frac{-10\%}{10\%} \right| = 1.0 \quad \text{(intermediate response)}\]

Why Do We Use Percentages?

We use percentage changes when calculating the price elasticity of demand because it is the percentage change and not the absolute change in price that influences consumer behavior. For example, if the price of a Big Mac increases by $1, from $5 to $6, consumers may perceive this as a relatively large change. As a result, they may switch to alternatives, such as Burger King’s Whopper.

Now, suppose that the price of a BMW increases by $1, from $50,000 to $50,001. This will probably not have much of a change at all on the quantity demanded. This is because the percentage change in the price was not very large.

The Midpoint Formula

You calculate elasticity over an interval by calculating the percentage change in price and the percentage change in quantity. That is,

\[E_D = \left| \frac{\dfrac{\Delta Q_D}{\text{Midpoint } Q_D}}{\dfrac{\Delta P}{\text{Midpoint } P}} \right|\]

EXAM TIP: Midpoint Formula

You will almost definitely have a question that asks you to use the midpoint formula on the exam. Be sure this equation is on your notecard!

Note that, in this class, we use the midpoint of the starting and ending values in the denominator when calculating the percentage change.

So, for example, in the diagram below, the price elasticity of demand between points A and B can be calculated as follows:

\[\%\Delta Q_D = \frac{12 - 8}{10} = 40\% \qquad \%\Delta P = \frac{\$19 - \$21}{\$20} = -10\% \qquad E_D = \left| \frac{40\%}{-10\%} \right| = 4\]

As you can see, the elasticity of demand is 4, so demand is elastic between these two points.

Calculating Elasticity at a Point

So far, we have considered only elasticity over an interval. It is also possible to determine elasticity at a point along a demand curve. The formula for calculating the elasticity of demand at a point along the demand curve is as follows:

\[E_D = \left| \frac{\Delta Q_D}{\Delta P} \times \frac{P}{Q_D} \right|\]

EXAM TIP: Point Elasticity

Dr. Rush does not typically ask questions that require you to use this equation. He introduces it to show that the price elasticity of demand falls as you move down a straight-line demand curve.

We could use this equation to show that the price elasticity of demand falls as we move down a straight-line demand curve. Consider the following demand curve:

As you can see, the price elasticity of demand along a straight-line demand curve varies from 0 (at the horizontal intercept) to infinity (at the vertical intercept). At the vertical intercept, elasticity approaches infinity. At the horizontal intercept, elasticity is zero.

Technically, elasticity of demand is only defined at a point. However, we speak in general terms about how “elastic” entire curves are. Just know that there is a distinction.

Elasticity Examples

On an exam, you might be asked to calculate the price elasticity of demand given the percentage change in price and the percentage change in quantity. Alternatively, you might be asked to calculate the percentage change in quantity given the elasticity and the percentage change in price, or you might be asked to calculate the percentage change in price given the elasticity and the percentage change in quantity. Let’s explore each of these three possibilities.

Suppose that a 5% increase in the price causes a 15% decrease in the quantity demanded. What is the price elasticity of demand over this interval?

\[E_D = \left| \frac{\%\Delta Q_D}{\%\Delta P} \right| = \left| \frac{-15\%}{5\%} \right| = 3\]

Demand is elastic over this interval because the price elasticity of demand is greater than one.

Suppose that the price elasticity of demand is 2 and the price increases by 10%. What will happen to the quantity demanded as a result?

\[2 = \left| \frac{\%\Delta Q_D}{10\%} \right| \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \%\Delta Q_D = 2 \times 10\% = -20\%\]

We know that an increase in price caused quantity demanded to fall because the law of demand states that the quantity demanded decreases when the price increases.

Suppose that you know that the price elasticity of demand is 0.2 and that the quantity demanded rises by 10%. What happens to the price?

\[0.2 = \left| \frac{10\%}{\%\Delta P} \right| \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \%\Delta P = \frac{10\%}{0.2} = -50\%\]

We know that an increase in quantity demanded caused the price to fall because the law of demand states that the quantity demanded increases when the price decreases.

The Total Revenue Test

Elasticity of demand is technically only defined at a point along the demand curve. The price elasticity of demand varies along a given demand curve. Along a given straight-line demand curve that intersects both the vertical and horizontal axes, the price elasticity of demand varies from zero (at the horizontal intercept) to infinity (at the vertical intercept). Revenues are always maximized at the midpoint of a straight-line demand curve, where the price elasticity of demand is equal to one. This relationship is shown in the figure below.

This relationship has important implications for firms wanting to raise their prices:

  • If demand is elastic, a firm will reduce its total revenues if it raises its price. It will increase its total revenues if it reduces its price.
  • If demand is inelastic, a firm will increase its total revenues if it raises its price. It will decrease its total revenues if it reduces its price.
  • If demand is unit elastic, a firm’s total revenues will not change if it raises or reduces its price, because the percentage change in the quantity demanded exactly offsets the percentage change in the price.

EXAM TIP: Total Revenue Test

The best way to remember the total revenue test is to remember just one relationship and change it up:

“If demand is inelastic, then increasing the price increases the total revenue.”

This can be changed any number of ways. For example, if you remember that rule, you can develop a rule for when demand is elastic:

“If demand is elastic, then increasing the price decreases the total revenue.”

You could also change this rule to reflect a decrease in the price and not an increase:

“If demand is elastic, then decreasing the price increases the total revenue.”

While the total revenue test may be confusing at first, if you just remember one simple rule and learn to change it so that it applies to different situations, you’ll be able to handle any question dealing with it on the exam.

Example: Elasticity and Piesanos

Elasticity is important because it helps companies decide whether they should raise prices. For example, let’s say that Piesanos is selling 1,000 pizzas at $10 per pizza, for a total revenue of $10,000, and that it is considering raising its price to $15 per pizza. The effect on Piesanos’ total revenue depends on how responsive customers are to the price hike. Piesanos would want to know what would happen to the quantity demanded if it took this action.

For example, there might be a strong response, and it might only sell 500 pizzas. If this happens, its new total revenue will be $15 × 500 = $7,500. In this case, Piesanos would probably not want to raise its price. However, there may not be a very large response at all. For example, Piesanos might sell 800 pizzas at $15 each, for a total revenue of $15 × 800 = $12,000. In this case, Piesanos probably would want to raise its price.

This is a simple example that illustrates how businesses use the concept of elasticity in the “real world.” Companies like Ford, Procter & Gamble, and Pfizer have teams of economists who do nothing but collect data to try to estimate elasticities!

Determinants of the Price Elasticity of Demand

The following factors determine the extent to which demand is elastic:

  • Number of close substitutes – The larger the number of close substitutes the product has, the more elastic the demand for it will be. This is the key factor. For example, to a diabetic, there are no good substitutes for insulin (a life-saving drug). Therefore, his or her demand for insulin will be very inelastic. On the other hand, the demand for an individual farmer’s wheat is extremely elastic, because there are many substitutes (other farmers’ wheat).

    Three factors that influence how many close substitutes there are for a good are as follows:

    • How much time has passed since the change in price – The more time that has passed since the price changed, the more substitutes people will have discovered, and thus the more elastic the demand will be. For example, in the short run, gas may have an elasticity of demand of 0.2; you’re not going to change your life because of a short-run change in the price of gas. In the long run, gas may have an elasticity of demand of 0.6 because you may plan your life more around the change in the price of gas, such as by living closer to school or work, not using a car, or using a more fuel-efficient car.
    • How broadly or narrowly defined the good is – The more narrowly a good is defined, the more substitutes it has, and thus the more elastic the demand for it will be. For example, the demand for Pepsi Zero is a lot more elastic than the demand for soda in general. You can replace Pepsi with other kinds of soda, so there are many substitutes for this item. On the other hand, there are fewer substitutes for soda in general.
    • Luxury goods vs. necessities – The demand for luxury goods tends to be more elastic than the demand for necessities. For example, if the price of a Caribbean cruise doubles, your response would be stronger than your response to a doubling of the price of toothpaste. You need to have toothpaste if you want clean teeth; you don’t need to go on a cruise.
  • The fraction of budget spent on the good – The larger the fraction of a person’s budget spent on a good, the larger its elasticity of demand because there is more incentive to look for alternatives. For example, the demand for rent is more elastic than the demand for salt. Rent takes up much more of your income than salt, so you would notice a doubling in rent more than you would a doubling in the price of salt. In a snowy city like Rochester, New York, on the other hand, salt is a big part of the budget because the city has to salt the roads (around 4 or 5% of the city budget). These cities will pay more attention to the price of salt, and their demand for it will be more elastic.

Other Elasticity Measures

Recall that elasticity is a measure of responsiveness. Thus far, we’ve only examined the price elasticity of demand, which measures responsiveness of the quantity demanded to a change in the price. There are other elasticity measures, as well:

  • Income elasticity of demand (EINC) – The income elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded to a change in income. It is calculated as follows:

    \[E_{INC} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_D}{\%\Delta \text{Income}}\]

    Here, notice that the sign is meaningful—that is why we don’t take the absolute value (as we do with the price elasticity of demand). The income elasticity of demand is negative for inferior goods and positive for normal goods. For example, rental housing is an inferior good—the quantity of rental housing demanded falls as income rises because richer people tend to own their homes.

  • Cross-price elasticity of demand – The cross-price elasticity of demand measures the impact of a change in the price of one good on the quantity demanded of a related good. That is, the cross-price elasticity of demand between goods A and B is defined as follows:

    \[E_{A,B} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_{D,\text{Good A}}}{\%\Delta P_{\text{Good B}}}\]

    Here, the sign matters, as well. The cross-price elasticity of demand is positive for substitutes and negative for complements, by definition. Consider the following examples:

    • Hamburgers and hot dogs – Hamburgers and hot dogs are substitutes, so the cross-price elasticity of demand between these goods is positive.
    • Hamburger meat and hamburger buns – Hamburger meat and hamburger buns are complements, so the cross-price elasticity of demand between these goods is negative.
  • Price elasticity of supply – The price elasticity of supply is a measure of the sensitivity of quantity supplied to a change in price. It is defined as follows:

    \[E_S = \frac{\%\Delta Q_S}{\%\Delta P}\]

    Note that the elasticity of supply will always be positive, as the law of supply says that the quantity supplied increases with positive increases in price. Thus, there is no need to take an absolute value. The price elasticity of supply is described in the same way we describe the price elasticity of demand. Supply is elastic if E > 1, inelastic if E < 1, and unit elastic if E = 1. Likewise, supply is perfectly inelastic if E = 0 and perfectly elastic if E = ∞.
