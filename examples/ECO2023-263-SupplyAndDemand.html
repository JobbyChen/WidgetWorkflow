Introduction to Supply and Demand

A market is an arrangement in which buyers and sellers can transact business. A competitive market is one with lots of buyers and lots of sellers, so no one person or firm determines the price. The best way to divide markets is between the demanders (buyers) and the suppliers (sellers). For most goods and services, we think of households as the demanders and firms as the suppliers. However, for some markets, like the labor market, it is just the opposite: Firms demand labor from households that supply it.

Demand

The law of demand says there is an inverse relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded. That is, as the price of a good goes up, the quantity demanded will decrease. The law of demand is depicted in the negative slope of the demand curve, which shows the relationship between the price and the quantity demanded.

Be aware that supply and demand curves don’t have to be linear (straight lines). They can be curved, and they almost always are. Unless it is absolutely essential that they be shown as curves, these notes will present supply and demand linearly, for simplicity and ease of understanding.

Note that a change in demand is completely different from a change in quantity demanded (QD). A movement along the demand curve represents a change in quantity demanded. A complete shift of the demand curve represents a change in demand.

Factors That Shift the Demand Curve

The following are factors that cause the entire demand curve to shift:

  1. Change in Income – Income affects different goods in different ways. There are two types of goods—normal goods and inferior goods.
    • A normal good is a good for which an increase in income causes an increase in demand (a rightward shift of the demand curve). Alternatively, a decrease in income causes a decrease in demand (a leftward shift of the demand curve). Most goods—including pizza and steak—are normal goods.
    • An inferior good is a good for which an increase in income causes a decrease in demand (a leftward shift of the demand curve). Alternatively, a decrease in income causes an increase in demand (a rightward shift of the demand curve). Examples of inferior goods include ramen noodles, low-end fast food, boxed mac & cheese, used cars, and inter-city bus travel.
  2. Change in the Price of a Related Good – Changes in prices of different types of goods affect the demand differently. There are two types of related goods (in consumption)—substitutes and complements.
    • Substitutes (in consumption) are goods that are used in place of each other. When two goods are substitutes, an increase in the price of one causes an increase in the demand for the other. Alternatively, a decrease in the price of one causes a decrease in the demand for the other. Examples include Coke and Pepsi and tacos and pizza.
    • Complements (in consumption) are goods that are bought separately but used together. When two goods are complements, an increase in the price of one causes a decrease in the demand for the other. Alternatively, a decrease in the price of one causes an increase in the demand for the other. Examples include hot dogs and hot dog buns, golf balls and golf clubs, and pizza and beer.

      Sometimes, the mere presence of complements increases the demand for a product. For example, the demand for iPhones is much higher because of the existence of valuable iPhone apps that can only be accessed with iPhones.

  3. Change in Preferences – Demand increases when consumers come to like the product more, and it decreases when consumers come to like the product less. New information can change consumer preferences. For example, if consumers discover that eating salmon is healthier than once believed, the demand for salmon might increase. Likewise, Taylor Swift is so popular that if she starts saying that she really loves pizza, the demand for pizza will increase.
  4. Change in the Number of Demanders – Demand increases when the number of demanders increases, and it decreases when the number of demanders decreases. For example, the population of older people who need medical care is increasing as the baby boomers get old. This will cause an increase in the demand for pharmaceutical drugs. Likewise, the demand for everything goes down in Gainesville during the summer because many students go home.
  5. Credit Market Conditions/State of Financial Markets – If credit conditions are good, demand will rise. If credit conditions are bad, demand will fall. This applies most strongly to big-ticket items like automobiles, houses, and appliances.
  6. Change in Expected Future Price – Demand also depends on what you think the price of a good will be in the future. Demand will increase today if people think that the price will increase in the future, and it will fall today if people think it will fall in the future. For example, people will buy gas now if they think its price will rise in the future. This is a more important factor for durable goods than for perishables.

Supply

The law of supply says that as the price of a good goes up, the quantity supplied will also increase. The quantity supplied changes as the price of a product changes. Taking the pizza example that we used previously, the higher the price of pizza, the more pizzas a producer would supply because he or she would be able to make more profit from producing and selling more pizza. The law of supply is depicted in the positive slope of the supply curve, which shows the relationship between the price and the quantity supplied.

Again, a change in supply is completely different from a change in quantity supplied (QS). A movement along the supply curve represents a change in quantity supplied. A complete shift of the supply curve represents a change in supply. A change in supply is caused by any relevant factor except the price of the good itself.

Factors That Shift The Supply Curve

The following factors cause a change in supply, or a shift of the supply curve. Most of these factors affect a firm’s profit. In general, the more profitable production is, the more firms will produce. The following are the most important factors that shift the supply curve:

  1. Costs of Production – Higher costs make production less profitable, so firms will respond by reducing supply. For example, an increase in the cost of producing Piesanos pizza will cause the supply curve for Piesanos pizza to shift to the left. This means that the quantity supplied will be lower at every price. Likewise, if the price of the corn that is fed to chickens increases, the supply of chickens falls.

    Note that the cost of production is not the same as the price. The cost of production is what it costs the producer to produce the product. The price is what the producer charges (and the buyer pays) for the product.

  2. Prices of Substitutes and Complements in Production – Automakers can make SUVs, or they can make hybrid cars. These are substitutes in production. If SUVs rise in price, the supply of hybrids will fall. The reason is that automakers will devote more of their resources to producing higher-priced SUVs when the price of SUVs increases.

    An example of complements in production is gasoline and diesel. When you refine oil, you automatically produce gasoline, fuel oil, diesel, and asphalt as byproducts. So if the price of diesel fuel goes up, then more gasoline will be made, and the supply of gasoline will rise. Likewise, when you produce chicken breasts, you automatically also produce chicken wings. Chicken breasts and chicken wings are therefore complements in production as well.

  1. Technology – Generally, we just think of technological advances. Increases in technology make it easier to produce a good, which causes a rightward shift of the supply curve for that good. A technological regression would make it more difficult to produce goods, which would cause the supply curve to shift to the left. Technological change has played a particularly crucial role in the electronics and automotive industries. For example, advances in computer chip technologies have increased the supply of many goods.
  2. Number of Suppliers – As the number of suppliers goes up, so does the supply. Likewise, supply falls when the number of suppliers falls. For example, if the number of pizza producers in the United States increases, the supply of pizza increases.
  3. State of Nature – This is a broad concept that includes natural events, such as hurricanes, earthquakes, floods, tsunamis, droughts, and pandemics. A positive state of nature causes supply to increase, whereas a negative state of nature causes supply to decrease. For example, in 2017 Hurricane Maria hit Puerto Rico and devastated production facilities there. Another example is the 2011 earthquake and tsunami in Japan, which disrupted production and supply chains (including the Fukushima nuclear disaster), reducing the economy’s ability to produce goods and services in the short run.
  4. Expected Future Price – If the expected future price is higher, the producer will supply less right now and wait until the future (when the price is higher) to increase the supply. This is particularly important for a few products (such as gasoline and oil).

Equilibrium, Shortages, and Surpluses

Your textbook defines equilibrium as a situation in which opposing forces (like supply and demand) are in balance. Dr. Rush describes equilibrium as “a situation in which there is no automatic tendency for change.”

The equilibrium price will be set at the point where the supply curve and the demand curve intersect. If the price is set either higher or lower than this price, market forces will cause the price to fall or rise until it reaches equilibrium. At the equilibrium price, the quantity demanded is equal to the quantity supplied, and there is neither a shortage nor a surplus.

If the price is something other than the equilibrium price, a shortage or surplus will result:

  • A shortage occurs when quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. This occurs when the price of the good or service is less than the equilibrium price. When a shortage situation occurs, the price will automatically tend to rise toward equilibrium.
  • A surplus occurs when quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded. This occurs when the price of the good or service is greater than the equilibrium price. When a surplus situation occurs, the price will automatically tend to decrease toward equilibrium.

Example: The Market for Shoes

Suppose that the price of shoes is set below the equilibrium price. The result will be a temporary shortage, because quantity demanded will be greater than quantity supplied. In this situation, buyers will rush to stores (for example, shoe stores at Butler Plaza) to try to purchase shoes at the low price. However, because shoes are selling out quickly and many buyers are leaving empty-handed, suppliers will raise their prices. They will continue to raise their prices as long as they are selling all of the shoes they have available. That is, they will continue to raise the price until they reach market equilibrium – where quantity demanded is exactly equal to quantity supplied.

Now, let’s assume that profit-seeking shoe sellers set the price very high – at $200 per pair. At that price, a lot of people just won’t want to buy shoes – either they won’t be able to afford them or they will be content with substitutes (such as waiting longer to replace their shoes or buying a different type of footwear). In this case, we will observe a surplus, where the quantity supplied is greater than the quantity demanded. Shoe sellers, seeing that their shoes are not being bought and inventory is piling up, will be forced to lower prices. They will continue lowering the price until they reach the point where they are selling all of their shoes. This is equilibrium, the point where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded.

Finally, let’s assume that the price of shoes is set at the equilibrium price. At this point, there will be no surplus and no shortage. Quantity supplied is exactly equal to quantity demanded. Therefore, there will be no upward or downward pressure on the price. Every pair of shoes that suppliers bring to the market will be bought by a buyer, and no buyer who is willing to pay the market price will be left without shoes. What we are seeing is an equilibrium, where the opposing forces of supply and demand balance each other out.

Illustrating Shifts of the Demand and Supply Curves

When one factor changes and you expect a shift in either the supply or demand curve, the following steps will help you determine what will happen to the price and the quantity:

  1. Draw the supply and demand diagram and label everything.
  2. Determine if the factor shifts the demand curve or the supply curve.
  3. Determine the direction of the shift.
  4. Draw the new curve to determine the new price and quantity.

You may be able to reason intuitively what happens to price and quantity, but it’s best to follow these steps. Always draw a diagram during a test—use your intuition as a backup to confirm what the diagrams tell you.

Example: The Market for Wheat

Ukraine is a major producer of wheat. Suppose you’re the manager of a large cereal producer, and you’re responsible for buying wheat. Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022. What should you have done then?

This is how you would systematically answer this question:

  1. Draw the supply and demand diagram and label everything.
  2. Determine if the factor shifts the demand curve or the supply curve. The Russian invasion of Ukraine decreased the number of suppliers of wheat. This is a factor that shifts the supply curve.
  3. Determine the direction of the shift. A decrease in the number of suppliers of wheat is a factor that shifts the supply curve for wheat to the left. (The demand curve does not shift because a change in the number of suppliers does not affect the demand curve.)
  4. Draw the new curve to determine the new price and quantity.

As you can see, the equilibrium price increases and the equilibrium quantity decreases as a result of the reduction in the number of wheat suppliers. (This scenario actually happened. The price of wheat dramatically increased after Russia invaded Ukraine. If you were a manager at a major cereal producer and you were in charge of buying wheat, you would have wanted to respond by quickly securing long-term contracts to buy wheat at the lower price.)

Shifts Due to a Change in Two Factors

If you have two factors that change at the same time (resulting in a shift of both the demand and supply curves), it is more complicated to determine what the overall effect on the price and quantity will be. The steps for solving a problem like this are:

  1. Draw two supply and demand diagrams, and label everything.
  2. Determine which curve shifts for each factor that changes.
  3. Determine the direction of each shift.
  4. On the first diagram, draw a big shift in demand and a small shift in supply. On the second diagram, draw a big shift in supply and a small shift in demand.

For cases when both the demand and supply curves shift and you are not told about the magnitude of either shift, you will know unambiguously about the effect on one of the two—price or quantity. The effect on the other will be ambiguous. If you know that one of the shifts is larger than the other, you will know unambiguously what happens to both price and quantity.

EXAM TIP: When One Shift Is Bigger

On an exam, you might be told that one shift is bigger than the other. In this case, you will be able to tell unambiguously how the price and quantity will change. You will only have to draw one diagram.

Example: The Market for Chicken Wings

Suppose that chicken wings are a normal good and people’s incomes increase. At the same time, the price of corn (used to feed chickens) increases. What happens to the equilibrium price and quantity of chicken wings?

  1. Draw two supply and demand diagrams, and label everything.
  2. Determine which curve shifts for each factor that changes. A change in income is a factor that shifts the demand curve. A change in the cost of production is a factor that shifts the supply curve.
  3. Determine the direction of each shift. An increase in income causes the demand curve for a normal good to shift to the right. An increase in the cost of production causes the supply curve to shift to the left.
  4. On the first diagram, draw a big shift in demand and a small shift in supply. On the second diagram, draw a big shift in supply and a small shift in demand.

    In this case, we can tell that the equilibrium price will definitely increase. However, we can’t be so sure about the quantity. In one diagram, the quantity increased, and in the other, the quantity decreased. To be able to tell what the quantity will do, we need to know which is greater: the shift in the supply curve or the shift in the demand curve. If, however, they shift the same amount, then the price might not change at all. Since we are not given this information, the best thing we can say is that we know for a fact that equilibrium price increases, but that the equilibrium quantity may either decrease, increase, or stay the same.

Review: Effects of a Shift of Two Curves on Equilibrium Price and Quantity

When the supply and demand curves shift simultaneously, you will always unambiguously know what happens to either price or quantity, and the change in the other will be ambiguous.

  • When both supply and demand shift right, quantity definitely rises, and the change in price is ambiguous.
  • When both supply and demand shift left, quantity definitely falls, and the change in price is ambiguous.
  • When supply shifts right and demand shifts left, price definitely falls, and the change in quantity is ambiguous.
  • When supply shifts left and demand shifts right, price definitely rises, and the change in quantity is ambiguous.

Note that you would unambiguously know what happens to price and quantity if you knew the relative magnitude of the shifts. For example, suppose that you were told that the demand curve and the supply curve shifted to the left, and the impact of the leftward shift of the demand curve was greater than the impact of the leftward shift of the supply curve. The supply curve still shifts to the left, and the demand curve still shifts to the left. However, because the leftward shift of the demand curve is greater than the leftward shift of the supply curve, we can unambiguously tell that the equilibrium price will decrease and the equilibrium quantity will decrease.

From the Textbook: Topic Not Covered

The price listed on supply and demand diagrams is technically a relative price, or a price relative to the price of other goods. This is different from a money price, or a price in terms of dollars.

For example, suppose that the price of turkey is 24 cents per pound. You may initially think this is cheap because the price of steak is currently much higher than that. However, if you were told that the price of steak was just 6 cents per pound, you’d probably think of turkey as being expensive! (Interestingly, this is how it was in the 1800s. The price of turkey was much higher than the price of steak, so the relative price of turkey was very high.)
