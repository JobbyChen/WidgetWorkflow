Introduction to Global Trade

A country's exports are those goods that it sends to other countries, whereas its imports are those goods that it takes in from the rest of the world. In a trade relationship between two countries, one country exports goods, and another imports them. For example, the United States imports goods from the rest of the world, and it exports goods to the rest of the world.

The balance of trade is the difference between the value of exports and the value of imports. Since 1981, the United States has imported more than it has exported. (The balance of trade deficit was about -$950 billion in 2025.) It has financed this differential by borrowing from the rest of the world. As a result, the United States is now one of the world's largest net debtor nations. This issue and others (including exchange rates) are primarily macroeconomic issues that we will not discuss in this class. We will, however, analyze how international trade affects domestic markets.

The major reason for world trade is comparative advantage. Some countries are better at producing certain goods than others. That is, one country might be able to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country. All countries benefit when they specialize in producing the goods that they're good at producing and then trade with each other. What a country is good at producing depends, in part, on its endowments (in land, technology, natural resources, cheap labor, etc.).

When a good's world price is greater than its domestic price, the domestic country will benefit from exporting the good. When a good's world price is less than its domestic price, the domestic country will benefit from importing the good.

As we will see in this section, economists generally favor international trade because it increases the total surplus to society.

How to Determine Whether a Country Is a Net Importer or Exporter

When a nation opens its borders to trade, we can tell whether it will be a net importer or a net exporter of a good by comparing the world price with the domestic price.

  • When the domestic price is greater than the world price, the nation will be a net importer of the good. The quantity that the country will import is equal to the difference between the domestic quantity demanded and the domestic quantity supplied at the world price.
  • When the domestic price is less than the world price, the nation will be a net exporter of the good. The quantity that the country will export is equal to the difference between the domestic quantity supplied and the domestic quantity demanded at the world price.

Of course, if the domestic price equals the world price, the nation will be neither a net importer nor a net exporter of the good.

Focus on Exports

Consider a case in which the domestic price without trade is less than the world price. As the discussion above indicates, the nation in this case would be a net exporter of the good to the rest of the world. For example, the United States might export financial services to the rest of the world if it can provide those services at a lower price than the rest of the world.

Remember that we assumed that a country can sell as many units as it wants at the world price. Allowing trade therefore drives the domestic price up to the world price, and it forces domestic consumers to pay higher prices for their units. At the higher price, the domestic quantity supplied is higher, and the domestic quantity demanded is lower. The difference between the domestic quantity supplied and the domestic quantity demanded is the number of units that the country will export.

Here is a summary of the impacts of allowing trade in this market:

  • Impact on domestic consumers – Domestic consumers are worse off with trade. Their consumer surplus decreases for two reasons: (1) Fewer consumers get the good, and (2) consumers are forced to buy the good at the higher world price.
  • Impact on domestic producers – Domestic producers are better off with trade. Their producer surplus increases for two reasons: (1) They sell more units, and (2) they can charge the higher world price.
  • Impact on society as a whole – Society as a whole is better off with trade. The gain in producer surplus exceeds the loss in consumer surplus, so total surplus—the sum of producer and consumer surplus—increases in this market after trade.

Focus on Imports

Consider a case in which the domestic price without trade is greater than the world price. As the discussion above indicates, the nation in this case would be a net importer of the good from the rest of the world. For example, the United States is a net importer of smart phones from the rest of the world. We could certainly make smart phones ourselves, but we import these goods because other countries can make smart phones at a lower price. In this sense, the world price is less than the domestic price.

Remember that we assumed that a country can buy as many units as it wants at the world price. Allowing trade therefore drives the domestic price down to the world price, and it forces domestic producers to accept the lower world price for their units. At the lower price, the domestic quantity supplied is lower and the domestic quantity demanded is higher. The difference between the domestic quantity demanded and the domestic quantity supplied is the number of units that the country will import.

Here is a summary of the impacts of allowing trade in this market:

  • Impact on domestic consumers – Domestic consumers are better off with trade. Their consumer surplus increases for two reasons: (1) More consumers get the good, and (2) consumers are able to buy the good at the lower world price.
  • Impact on domestic producers – Domestic producers are worse off with trade. Their producer surplus decreases for two reasons: (1) They sell fewer units, and (2) they have to accept the lower world price.
  • Impact on society as a whole – Society as a whole is better off with trade. The gain in consumer surplus exceeds the loss in producer surplus, so total surplus—the sum of producer and consumer surplus—increases in this market after trade.

Tariffs and Quotas

As we have discussed, trade has winners and losers. The "losers" often lobby the government for trade protection—a mechanism that restricts trade in an attempt to protect certain constituencies. In this section, we will focus on cases in which the domestic price is higher than the world price and the country is a net importer of the good. When this is the case, free trade harms domestic producers, who often lobby the government for protection. This protection comes in many forms, but we will focus on tariffs and quotas. (Note that there are other non-tariff barriers that restrict trade, as well. For example, excessive paperwork or inspection requirements can effectively increase the cost of imports without explicitly applying a tariff.)

Tariffs

A tariff is a tax that the government imposes on every unit that is imported. The imposition of a tariff effectively increases the domestic price relative to the world price. This reduces the domestic quantity demanded, increases the domestic quantity supplied, and reduces the amount that the nation imports.

Note that tariffs that are too high will completely eliminate imports. Specifically, a tariff that is as large as or larger than the difference between the domestic price and the world price will completely wipe out all imports. When this is the case, the domestic market will exclusively provide the goods at the domestic equilibrium price.

Here is a summary of the impacts of imposing a tariff in this market:

  • Impact on domestic consumers – Domestic consumers are made worse off by the tariff because they have to pay a higher price for the good, so they consume less of it. As a result, the domestic consumer surplus falls.
  • Impact on domestic producers – Domestic producers are made better off by the tariff because they can charge a higher price for the good, so they sell more of it. As a result, the domestic producer surplus rises.
  • Impact on the government – The government is made better off by the tariff because it gains tariff revenue. For example, if the tariff is $2 per unit and the quantity of goods imported is 20 million units, the government gains $40 million in tariff revenue.
  • Impact on society as a whole – Society as a whole is made worse off by the imposition of the tariff. Specifically, society loses an area of economic surplus equal to the darkened areas in the graph above. These areas are referred to as the deadweight loss that results from the imposition of a tariff.

Tariffs are representative of other restraints on trade, such as quotas, in that they cause a net loss to society. This is why most economists tend to favor measures that promote free trade and stand against measures that impede it.

If these restrictions on trade harm society, why do governments impose them? Critics of free trade often argue that importing goods costs domestic workers their jobs. This is a highly visible consequence of free trade, which tends to be emphasized in the media. An even more important consequence, as we have seen, is that domestic consumers benefit from lower prices. This is a less visible consequence of imports, however. Therefore, politicians have the incentive to listen more closely to opponents of free trade than to its proponents. Moreover, there are fewer suppliers than demanders, so they can more easily collaborate to lobby for tariffs.

Those who argue for trade restrictions are often engaged in rent seeking—attempting to gain a larger share of the economic pie without increasing the size of the pie. When domestic producers argue for tariffs on imported goods, they are trying to gain producer surplus at the expense of the rest of society.

For roughly 80 years after the Great Depression, the United States worked to reduce its tariffs. That trend has reversed over the past decade. Tariffs rose during President Trump's first administration, changed very little under President Biden, and rose again during President Trump's second administration. The average U.S. tariff is now roughly 12% to 14%, and even small tariffs can cause losses to society. For example, the United States currently imposes a tariff on Canadian steel: Steel produced in Canada is taxed the moment it crosses the border into the United States, whereas steel produced in the United States is not.

The Smoot-Hawley Tarriff

The Smoot-Hawley Tariff was a law that implemented protectionist trade policies in the United States, raising U.S. tariffs on more than 20,000 imported goods in 1930. This prompted retaliatory tariffs by other countries against the United States. As a result, American exports and imports fell by two-thirds during the Great Depression. While the Smoot-Hawley Tariff did not cause the Great Depression, it certainly made the Great Depression worse than it otherwise would have been.

Why Do Protectionist Policies Persist?

In this section, we have shown that free trade increases total surplus, but that there are winners and losers. For example, allowing imports increases domestic consumer surplus but it decreases domestic producer surplus. The increase in domestic consumer surplus exceeds the decrease in domestic producer surplus, so total domestic surplus increases as a result.

Protectionist policies (such as tariffs and quotas) exist because certain groups have a greater incentive to lobby for those policies than others. For example, suppose that allowing imports in an industry increases consumer surplus by $350 million and reduces producer surplus by $100 million. Clearly, society would be better off to allow imports because the total gain to society is greater than the total loss to society by $250 million.

However, suppose that there are 350 million consumers and only 10 large producers. The consumers stand to lose $1 each if trade is disallowed, while the producers stand to gain $10 million each if trade is disallowed. Clearly, producers have a greater incentive than consumers to lobby the government for protectionist policies that would disallow trade in this industry.

From the Textbook: Topics Not Covered

Here are some concepts the textbook covers that are not covered here:

  • Outsourcing occurs when U.S. firms buy finished goods, components, or services either from other U.S. firms or from firms in other countries. Offshoring occurs when U.S. firms produce in other countries using foreign labor or buy finished goods, components, or services from firms in other countries.
  • Three common arguments against free trade are as follows:
    • The infant-industry argument asserts that governments must protect new domestic industries from foreign competition so that those industries can properly develop.
    • The dumping argument points out that exporters can eliminate domestic producers by dumping—selling exports at a price below the cost of production.
    • The national defense argument asserts that certain industries (such as the steel or energy industries) are essential for a country's security and should be protected from foreign competition.
