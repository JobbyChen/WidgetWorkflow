Introduction to Efficiency

Allocative efficiency occurs when we produce the most highly valued combination of goods and services possible. In this chapter, we will see how buyers and sellers interact in markets in a way that achieves the allocatively efficient result for society.

Marginal Analysis

The word marginal means "additional." Marginal benefit (MB) is the benefit from one more unit. It's not the same as the total benefit, which is the benefit you get from the entire action. Marginal cost (MC) is the cost of one more unit. It's not the same as the total cost, which is the cost incurred from the entire action.

Marginal analysis compares the additional benefit of an action (marginal benefit) to the additional cost of the action (marginal cost). The rules of marginal analysis are as follows:

  • If \(MB > MC\), then you do the action.
  • If \(MB < MC\), then you don't do the action.

Example: Marginal Analysis and the Director

Suppose the director has already eaten five Big Macs and is now thinking about eating a sixth. The price of a Big Mac is $6, so the marginal cost of the sixth Big Mac to the director is $6. If the additional benefit of that Big Mac is greater than $6, the director will buy it. If the additional benefit of that Big Mac is less than $6, the director will not buy it.

Notice that it's not the total cost that the director worries about; it is the additional cost of the sixth Big Mac. For example, the total cost of six Big Macs is $36, but the marginal cost of the sixth Big Mac is $6. It's the marginal cost that we use, and not the total cost, when deciding whether the director will buy another Big Mac.

Dr. Rush emphasized that marginal analysis is very important in economics and in life. We will implicitly use marginal analysis throughout the rest of this course when we decide such things as whether a firm should produce more or less of a good and whether a firm should stay open or shut down. You could also use marginal analysis to make better everyday decisions in life.

Marginal Benefit and Marginal Cost Curves

The marginal benefit curve (MB) is the same as the demand curve. For example, the marginal benefit associated with the consumption of pizza is shown below. This curve tells us that $15 is the maximum price that someone will pay for the 4 millionth pizza. Therefore, this is the marginal benefit that that consumer receives from consuming the 4 millionth pizza. This is also the marginal social benefit (MSB) because the consumer is a member of society. (There are no external costs or benefits in this case, so the marginal private benefit is the same as the marginal social benefit. When we discuss externalities later in the course, we will see that this isn't always the case.)

Notice that as quantity demanded increases, the marginal benefit associated with an additional pizza decreases. The 12 millionth pizza, for example, is only worth $12 to society. This is logical; if you're really hungry, the first pizza is much more valuable to you than the fifth pizza.

The marginal cost curve (MC) is the same as the supply curve. For example, the marginal cost associated with the production of pizza is shown below. This curve tells us that $8 is the minimum price for which someone is willing to produce and sell the 2 millionth pizza. This is a good measure of the marginal cost of producing the 2 millionth pizza (which includes a little profit as incentive for production). This is also a good measure of the marginal social cost (MSC) of producing the pizza because the producer is a member of society. (Again, there are no externalities, so the marginal private cost is the same as the marginal social cost.)

Note that the marginal cost increases as more units are produced. This reflects the law of increasing opportunity cost—as we produce more of a good, the opportunity cost of producing an additional unit increases.

Using Marginal Analysis to Find the Allocatively Efficient Quantity

We can use marginal analysis to find the allocatively efficient quantity, or the quantity of a good that society should produce to maximize welfare. Specifically, the allocatively efficient quantity is the quantity for which the marginal social benefit equals the marginal social cost. In the absence of externalities, this quantity is determined by the intersection of the demand curve and supply curve.

Note that at the point where the marginal social cost is equal to the marginal social benefit, \(Q_{EFF} = Q*\) (the efficient quantity produced is also the equilibrium quantity). This says that the amount that will actually be produced will also be the amount that is best for society. An "invisible" hand causes equilibrium to automatically be the same point as the most efficient point.

Example: Allocative Efficiency and Pizza

The diagram below shows the marginal social benefit and marginal social cost curves associated with the worldwide consumption of pizza. At a level of one pizza, we're not at a socially efficient level of production and consumption. This is because \(MSB > MSC\). At this level, the additional benefit of producing that extra pizza is greater than the costs involved. From a social perspective, we definitely want to produce that pizza. This is because we have a surplus of $8. The pizza has a benefit to society that exceeds the costs to society by $8.

It turns out that we would definitely want to produce all of the pizzas right up to the equilibrium point. This is because each point left of the equilibrium price involves a surplus. That is, marginal social benefit is greater than marginal social cost. Even though the added surplus decreases as you produce more, you will still produce the extra pizza because the surplus you get is added surplus. Thus, your total surplus is still increasing. Total surplus is the shaded region in the diagram below.

This supports our marginal analysis theory. When marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost, you continue to do the activity. That is, you produce. When marginal benefit is less than the marginal cost, you don't produce the pizza. Your optimal production point will be at equilibrium, where \(MSB = MSC\). This is the quantity at which the total net surplus to society is maximized, also called the allocatively efficient point.

Here, we see that people acting in their own self-interest are led, as if by an invisible hand, to do what's best for society. Adam Smith, the father of economics, came up with a theory in his 1776 book, The Wealth of Nations, called the invisible hand theory. This states that people looking out for only their own self-interest are led, as if by an invisible hand, to do what's best for society. Consumers will behave in a way that makes them as well-off as possible, and producers will do the same. In the absence of externalities, the interaction of self-interested consumers and self-interested producers will lead society to allocate resources in a way that maximizes total surplus to society.

Consumer Surplus, Producer Surplus, and Total Surplus

We have discussed that \(Q_{EFF}\) is the most efficient point—the point at which there will be maximum surplus to society. In that case, producing at \(Q_{EFF}\) will cause the total surplus to be the area between the demand curve and supply curve, up to the equilibrium quantity. How is that divided?

  • Consumer surplus is the maximum amount someone is willing to pay minus the actual price. \((MSB - Price)\)
  • Producer surplus is the price actually received for the product minus the minimum price for which the producer will sell it. \((Price - MSC)\)
  • Total surplus is equal to consumer surplus plus producer surplus \((CS + PS)\)

Note that we maximize total surplus at the point where the marginal social cost is equal to the marginal social benefit—that is, where \(Q_{EFF} = Q*\) (the efficient quantity produced is also the equilibrium quantity).

Example: Calculating Total Surplus

Let's look at just who gets the surplus generated by the sale of Piesanos' pizza. Given the demand and supply curves below, let's assume that the equilibrium price of a pizza is $11.

The consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve and above the price, to the left of the equilibrium quantity. The producer surplus is the area below the price and above the supply curve, to left of the equilibrium quantity.

The total consumer surplus can be calculated as follows:

CS = \(\frac{1}{2} \times \text{Base} \times \text{Height}\)
= \(\frac{1}{2} (8M) \times (\$21 - \$11)\) = $40 million

The total producer surplus can be calculated as follows:

PS = \(\frac{1}{2} \times \text{Base} \times \text{Height}\)
= \(\frac{1}{2} (8M) \times (\$11 - \$1)\) = $40 million

The total surplus is the sum of the consumer surplus and the producer surplus, or $80 million.

In this example, the consumer surplus was equal to the producer surplus. This isn't always the case, however. The consumer surplus could be greater than, equal to, or less than the producer surplus, depending on the elasticities of demand and supply.

It's important to note that, when you produce at the equilibrium price and quantity (the efficient price and quantity), you maximize total surplus. The concept of deadweight loss will illustrate why this is so.

Deadweight Loss

Total surplus to society is maximized when society produces the efficient quantity, which is also the equilibrium quantity in the absence of externalities. If society underproduces or overproduces with respect to the equilibrium quantity, the total surplus to society is smaller than if the efficient quantity were produced. The deadweight loss to society is the loss of total surplus due to underproduction or overproduction. Nobody gains from this loss; it is a total waste to society.

Example: Deadweight Loss and Pizza

Suppose that the equilibrium quantity of pizzas is 15 million and society, for some reason, only produces 10 million. The result will be a deadweight loss because society failed to produce 5 million pizzas for which the marginal social benefit exceeded the marginal social cost. Society could increase total surplus by producing more pizzas.

Now, suppose that society produces 20 million pizzas. Again, there will be a deadweight loss to society. This time, the deadweight loss results because society is producing 5 million pizzas for which the marginal social cost exceeds the marginal social benefit. Society could increase total surplus by producing fewer pizzas.

In both of these cases, the area that represents the difference between the maximum possible surplus (surplus achieved when producing at equilibrium) and the actual surplus (surplus achieved at the point where we produce) is called the deadweight loss.

From-the-Book Topics NOT Covered in Class

Here are some concepts covered in the textbook but not covered in class:

  • In the United States, we allocate resources through markets. There are alternatives, however. Examples include the command system, majority rule, contests, lotteries, personal characteristics, first-come/first-served systems, and force.
  • Utilitarianism is a principle that states that we should strive to achieve "the greatest happiness for the greatest number." Utilitarians argue that income must be transferred from the rich to the poor, up to the point of complete equality, in order to achieve this end.
  • The big trade-off refers to the trade-off between efficiency and fairness. John Rawls, in his A Theory of Justice (1971), argued that the fairest distribution of the economic pie is one that makes the poorest person as well off as possible.
  • The idea that it's not fair if the rules aren't fair is based on the symmetry principle, which is the requirement that people in similar situations be treated similarly. In economic life, this principle translates into equality of opportunity.
