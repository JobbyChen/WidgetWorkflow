Introduction to Government Intervention

In this section, we will discuss government intervention in markets. Specifically, we will consider three types of government intervention:

  • A price ceiling exists when the government sets the highest price that can legally be charged and paid for a good or service.
  • A price floor exists when the government sets the lowest price that can legally be charged and paid for a good or service.
  • A tax exists when the government requires an additional payment upon the sale of a good. (We will only consider excise taxes, which are taxes imposed on each unit of a good sold.)

We will see that all three of these government policies result in a loss of total surplus to society (i.e., a deadweight loss).

Price Controls: Price Ceilings and Price Floors

The government can impose a maximum price that can be charged for a good or service (a price ceiling) or a minimum price that can be charged for a good or service (a price floor).

Ceilings/Floors

Price ceilings and price floors can be easily confused because of how we picture "ceilings" and "floors" in our minds. Generally, price ceilings are prices set below the equilibrium price, and price floors are prices set above the equilibrium price, which may confuse you. On a test, be sure that you think your graphs out—don't necessarily trust your intuition.

Price Ceilings

A binding price ceiling is a price below the equilibrium price, at which it is illegal to charge a higher price. The imposition of a price ceiling redistributes surplus between consumers and producers and reduces total economic surplus. The quantity supplied at the price ceiling is less than the quantity demanded, and a shortage results. The quantity transacted will be the quantity supplied because it is the lower of the quantity supplied and the quantity demanded. The quantity transacted under a price ceiling is less than the quantity transacted at equilibrium.

The diagram below shows the impact of the imposition of a binding price ceiling on the market. At the ceiling price—which is below the equilibrium price—the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied, and a shortage results. The quantity transacted is the quantity supplied (because it is the lower of the quantity supplied and quantity demanded). A deadweight loss results because the quantity transacted is less than the equilibrium (efficient) quantity.

Demanders can deal with the shortage resulting from the price ceiling in two ways:

  1. They increase their search activity for the product, which is an inefficient waste of time.
  2. They create a "black market" for the product, in which it is illegally sold at a price higher than the ceiling price.

The only winners from price ceilings are demanders who get to buy the product at a lower price, with little or no search. The losers from price ceilings include suppliers (who have to accept a lower price for the product, if they get to sell the product at all), demanders who can no longer buy the product, and demanders who can only buy the product after a lot of search. On net, price ceilings harm society more than they help it, so they create a deadweight loss.

Rent Ceilings

Some cities (such as New York and Berkeley) have rent ceilings that determine the highest rent a landlord can charge a tenant. In the example below, the equilibrium rental rate is $2,000 per month, but the price ceiling is set at $1,500 per month. No landlord can charge more than $1,500 per month for rent. Therefore, there will be a shortage of apartments to rent, because QD > QS. Note that there is a deadweight loss because the quantity of apartments transacted is below the efficient (equilibrium) quantity.

Like all price ceilings, rent ceilings only have an impact on the market when they are set below the equilibrium price. For example, if the government imposed a rent ceiling of $1 billion per month, it would have no impact on the market because no apartment would be rented for $1 billion per month.

The rent ceiling would create a shortage of apartments, which will lead people to increase their search activity. Landlords may also ask for "side payments" from tenants that are above and beyond the maximum legal amount of rent. For example, renters may have to pay "key money"—an extra lump sum of money—just to get the key. In this sense, a black market is created due to the excess demand generated by the price ceiling. The same kind of thing happened with the gasoline shortage in the 1970s, when gas stations sold "special" packages including gas and an oil change for a price far in excess of what they would cost individually.

Note that price ceilings only have an impact on the market if they are set below the equilibrium price. That is, price ceilings set above equilibrium price will have no effect at all, and the equilibrium price and quantity will prevail. For example, if the government said that the maximum amount that could be paid for a pack of gum is $1 million, that law wouldn't impact the market at all (because no pack of gum would ever be sold for $1 million, anyway).

EXAM TIP

A price ceiling only has an impact on the quantity transacted if it is set below the equilibrium price.

Price Floors

A binding price floor is a price above the equilibrium price below which it is illegal to charge. The imposition of a price floor redistributes surplus between producers and consumers and reduces total economic surplus. The quantity supplied at the price floor is greater than the quantity demanded, and a surplus results. The lower of the quantity demanded and quantity supplied determines the quantity transacted, so the quantity demanded will be the quantity transacted. The quantity transacted under a price floor is less than the quantity transacted at equilibrium.

The diagram below shows the impact of the imposition of a binding price floor on the market. At the floor price—which is above the equilibrium price—the quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded, and a surplus results. The quantity transacted is the quantity demanded (because it is the lower of the quantity supplied and quantity demanded). A deadweight loss results because the quantity transacted is less than the equilibrium (efficient) quantity.

The only winners from price floors are suppliers who are able to sell the product for a higher price. The losers include all demanders, who either have to pay a higher price for the good or cannot get it at all, and suppliers who are not able to sell their product at the higher price. On net, price floors harm society more than they help it, so they create a deadweight loss.

Just as price ceilings only have an impact on the market if they are set below the equilibrium price, price floors only have an impact on the market if they are set above the equilibrium price. If a price floor is set below the equilibrium price, the equilibrium price and quantity will prevail. For example, if the government imposed a price floor of $2 on new MacBooks, it would have no impact on the market because MacBooks would never be sold for $2 or less.

When a price floor is imposed, it creates a surplus of the good. For example, price floors are common for agricultural products, as the government wants to ensure that domestic farmers earn a minimum amount for their crops. The government sometimes eliminates this surplus by buying the excess crops from farmers, which is essentially a subsidy to the farmers.

Example: The Minimum Wage

The minimum wage is a price floor in the labor market. It is illegal to pay workers below the minimum wage. This price floor on labor creates a surplus of labor, which we call unemployment.

The diagram below shows the impact of the imposition of a minimum wage. (Note that, in the market for labor, firms demand labor and households supply it. The wage rate is the price of labor.) When a $13 per hour minimum wage is set in a market with an equilibrium wage of $6 per hour, the quantity of labor supplied exceeds the quantity of labor demanded; this surplus of labor is called unemployment.

The quantity of labor transacted will be the quantity of labor demanded, which is less than the efficient (equilibrium) quantity. As a result, the minimum wage results in a deadweight loss to society.

Note that some demographic groups are disadvantaged by the minimum wage more than others. For example, college students are largely unaffected (directly) by the minimum wage. In contrast, the minimum wage likely significantly impacts certain minority groups, which is one reason why the unemployment rate for this demographic group is higher than average.

Example: Unions and the Minimum Wage

Unions support increases in the minimum wage because less-skilled labor is a substitute for higher-skilled union labor. Assume that two less-skilled workers can do what one more-productive union worker can do. The union wage is $25. If the minimum wage is $10, then employers might hire the two less-skilled workers (because hiring two less-skilled workers at the minimum wage would cost less than hiring one union worker). But if the minimum wage is $15, it makes sense for the employers to hire the union worker (because hiring the union worker at the union wage would cost less than hiring two less-skilled workers). This is why unions support increases in the minimum wage.

Why Price Floors and Price Ceilings?

If price floors and price ceilings make society lose, then why are they put in place at all? Those who stand to win from floors and ceilings lobby the government heavily for these floors and ceilings, while many of the people who stand to lose don't really care.

Example: Why Rent Ceilings Persist in New York City

As we have discussed, price ceilings create a deadweight loss, which means that the loss due to a price ceiling exceeds any gain that it produces. If deadweight losses are bad for society, why do they persist? For example, why do New York City's rent controls persist?

New York City's rent controls were imposed in the late 1940s, when the soldiers who fought in World War II returned home from Europe. These rent controls were imposed to ensure that these individuals were not priced out of the expensive housing market in New York City.

This justification for the rent controls is no longer valid, as all of the soldiers who were going to come back from fighting in World War II have done so. As a result, in 2000 there was a movement to remove New York City's rent controls. This movement ultimately failed, however, because tenants who benefited from the price controls lobbied heavily to keep them in place. Politicians who were in charge of the rent controls wanted to remain in office, so they listened to the tenants who benefited from the rent controls. The landlords were less numerous and more poorly organized than the tenants, so the tenants won.

This is an example of how lobbying and the political process can keep inefficient government policies in place.

Taxes

The government has to generate revenue to operate; one way it does so is through the imposition of taxes. When the government imposes a tax on a product, it is, in effect, increasing the cost of that product to the seller. Thus, an increase in taxes will shift the entire supply curve up by the amount of the tax.

The effect on the price will be that it will rise, but not by the entire amount of the tax. The new price (PNew) will be somewhere between the original price (POld) and the original price plus the tax (POld + Tax).

Note that the end result of a tax is the same whether the tax is imposed on the suppliers or demanders. In our discussion of taxes, we will assume that the tax is imposed on the suppliers. Also, in this class, we will focus on a per-unit tax, which is imposed on each unit sold. An alternative taxation scheme involves a percentage tax. If a percentage tax is imposed, the supply curve would not shift in a parallel fashion.

Example: Tax on Pizza in Gainesville

Consider the market for pizza in Gainesville. Let's assume that a pizza costs $12 without a tax. The government imposes a $1 tax on every pizza sold. The result will be an upward shift in the supply curve by the amount of the tax, which is shown in the diagram below.

Notice that the price did not change by the entire amount of the tax. Here, the producer pays 50 cents of the tax, and the consumer pays 50 cents of the tax. The net result of the tax is that, after paying the tax, suppliers now receive 50 cents less than what they used to receive before the tax, and buyers had to pay 50 cents more.

Why didn't the price rise to $13? Because, at that higher price, there would be a surplus of pizza. The price had to come down to get rid of the surplus. So, the suppliers had to give up 50 cents and the consumers had to give up 50 cents to make the $1 tax.

In this case, consumers and producers suffered equally due to the imposition of the tax. That is, the tax incidence is such that consumers pay the same amount of tax as producers do.

Taxes hurt society on the whole. This can be represented by the deadweight loss that we see created by the tax. We can look at the deadweight loss created by the imposition of a $1.00 tax on pizza. The deadweight loss is the area of the triangle between the initial equilibrium quantity and the quantity after the imposed tax, as seen above. The tax revenue is equal to the amount of the tax (here, $1 per unit), times the quantity sold under the tax.

Tax Incidence

Tax division (what the book refers to as tax incidence) refers to the division of the amount of the tax paid by the buyer and the supplier. The tax division depends on the elasticities of the supply and demand curves.

When the demand curve is more elastic, the share of a tax paid by consumers falls, and the share of the tax paid by producers rises. To illustrate this fact, let's examine the impact of an equivalent tax in markets where the demand curve is highly inelastic and highly elastic:

Think about it like this: Elasticity implies flexibility, or having more options. So, for example, if demanders have more options (i.e., if demand is highly elastic), they will not be willing to increase what they pay. As a result, the price will only rise a little.

In a case where demand is perfectly elastic, the supplier pays all of the tax. If demand is completely inelastic, the consumer pays all of the tax.

Market for Illegal Goods

Editor's note: Dr. Rush did not cover the market for illegal goods. This has historically been the most commonly asked-about "from-the-book" topic on Exam 1, so we are covering it in a separate section of the notes.

The effect of imposing legal restrictions on a given good or service depends on how the penalty is imposed.

  • Penalty imposed on sellers – Sometimes, penalties are imposed on sellers. For example, it is illegal to sell most recreational drugs in the United States. If the penalty is imposed on the sellers of the illegal good, this penalty amounts to an additional cost of production. As we have discussed, increased costs cause the supply to decrease, and the supply curve to shift to the left.
  • Penalty imposed on buyers – Other times, penalties are imposed on the buyers. For example, it is illegal to possess certain drugs in the United States. If the penalty is imposed on the buyers of the illegal good, this penalty amounts to a decrease in the value of the good to the consumer. This means that the demand curve will shift left and the demand for the illegal good will decrease.
  • Penalty imposed on both buyers and sellers – If penalties are imposed on both the buyers and the sellers, quite predictably, both the supply and demand curves would shift. The larger the penalties and the greater the degree of law enforcement, the larger the decrease in demand and/or supply:

    • In this case, we know unambiguously that the quantity of the illegal good transacted will fall, but we cannot tell the effect on the price until we know whether demand or supply decreases most.

In the United States, the penalties on sellers are larger than those on buyers, so the quantity of drugs traded decreases, and the market price increases compared with a free market.
