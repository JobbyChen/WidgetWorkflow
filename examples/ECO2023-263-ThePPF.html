The Production Possibilities Frontier

A production possibilities frontier (PPF) shows the maximum attainable combinations of two goods that can be produced with current technology and available resources. (You may also see it called a production possibilities curve; it is the same thing.) The PPF is the first abstract economic model we will use. For example, suppose that an economy can use its scarce resources to produce two goods: steaks or hamburgers. The PPF shows the maximum combinations of steaks and hamburgers that the nation can produce if it devotes all of its resources to producing those two goods. Dr. Rush builds his first frontier around a student who produces a GPA and dates (see the example below), and then around a simple economy that produces gumballs and machines.

  • Points on the PPF are attainable and efficient (Dr. Rush calls these production efficient points).
  • Points inside the PPF are attainable but inefficient (production inefficient points).
  • Points outside the PPF are unattainable—at least right now. If resources or technology increase, points that were unattainable may become attainable.

A society benefits from producing at a point on the PPF—that is, society benefits from producing the maximum possible combination of goods by working as efficiently as possible. An attainable and efficient point is always the most preferred. Note that you cannot ask whether a point beyond the PPF is efficient or inefficient—you have to be able to produce a point before you can judge whether it uses your resources efficiently.

The PPF helps us answer the “what” question and the “how” question, but not the “who” question. The PPF tells us what and how much a country can produce, but it does not tell us anything about how those goods and services are divided among the country’s citizens. Its answer to the “how” question is simply that we want to produce efficiently—on the PPF—without wasting or misallocating resources. In general, our capitalist economy does a good job at allocating resources, so the U.S. economy is likely operating near the PPF. Decades ago, China probably operated at a point well within its PPF, but it has moved closer to the PPF since adopting more capitalistic policies. Dr. Rush treats the PPF as a conceptual tool for organizing your thoughts—you cannot actually estimate a PPF—and after this chapter we will not see it again.

Scarcity & Opportunity Cost

The PPF reflects scarcity. Specifically, it reflects the fact that economies have limited technology and limited resources. People’s wants will always exceed the economy’s ability to meet them because resources are limited. The opportunity cost of an action or decision is the single next highest-valued alternative foregone because you took that action or made that decision. Dr. Rush calls this the “true” cost of an action. Almost every action you can think of has an opportunity cost. Even sitting in an economics course has an opportunity cost—the statistics course, the sleep, or the hours at work that you gave up to be there. In the real world, opportunity cost is difficult to measure, but it always exists; on a PPF it is easy to measure, because there are only two goods. The opportunity cost is the single next-best alternative foregone (i.e., a given action can have only one opportunity cost). An opportunity cost is the same thing as a relative price but different from a money cost. Money cost is expressed in dollars, whereas opportunity cost is expressed in terms of the things that you actually give up.

EXAM TIP: Opportunity Cost

Remember that the opportunity cost of an action or decision is the single next-highest valued alternative foregone.

Example: Food and Opportunity Cost

Suppose you have $10 to spend on lunch. You could choose between a Bento sushi roll, a Chick-fil-A sandwich, or a Panda Express bowl. Let’s say your preferences, from most to least desired, are:

  1. Bento sushi roll
  2. Chick-fil-A sandwich
  3. Panda Express bowl

If you buy the sushi roll, your opportunity cost is the foregone chance to enjoy the Chick-fil-A sandwich—your next-best alternative. The opportunity cost is not all the other options, just the single next-best one.

Example: A Student’s GPA

Let’s assume that a student does only two things: go on dates and study. He doesn’t eat, sleep, or do anything else. A student can get a GPA of 3.8 if he works his hardest and doesn’t go on any dates. He could go on 20 dates if he tries his hardest at it and doesn’t worry at all about school. Notice that points on the curve represent all of the maximum possible combinations of GPA and dates. Points under the curve are possible but not efficient—at those points, production would not be at maximum potential. Above the curve, production would be impossible at all points—the student simply can’t attain this combination of dates and GPA, no matter how hard he works. The PPF represents the maximum possible number of dates and GPA the student can attain by operating as efficiently as possible.

The opportunity cost is the next-best alternative foregone. Using our example, the student could change his grades and the number of dates he goes on—that is, he could move along the PPF. If he had a GPA of 2.0 and were going on 12 dates a week, he could change that. He could boost his GPA to 2.8, but he would have to give up 4 dates to do so, bringing him down to 8 dates a week. The 4 dates that he gave up represent the opportunity cost of increasing his GPA by 0.8. Similarly, if he wanted to go on more dates per week, he could do that, but his GPA would fall. The opportunity cost of going on more dates is the amount of grade points that he has to give up to do so.

EXAM TIP: Movement Along the PPF

If you’re at a point on your PPF, you can’t produce more of one good without giving up production of the other good.

When you’re operating inefficiently (i.e., from within the interior of the PPF curve), some of your resources are either unemployed or misallocated. In a case where you’re operating inefficiently, you can increase both your grades and your dates by operating more efficiently. Thus, there may not be an opportunity cost to adding more of either dates or grade points. A point within the curve is less desirable than a point on the curve because any point on the curve maximizes efficiency. Think of it like a test score: a 60% leaves room to improve while a 100% does not, but nobody prefers the 60%. Production inefficient points are the 60%—you could have more of everything—so you want to be at a production efficient point, and once you are there, changing means giving something up.

Time and skills are the two limitations on what this student can attain. He could shift the entire curve outward by getting better at one or both activities—getting dates and getting good grades. If he learned new study skills or improved his looks, he would have new capabilities, and his PPF would shift outward. Alternatively, if he got amnesia and couldn’t remember anything or he forgot to bathe, his PPF would shift inward—he would no longer be able to get grades as high as before, or he would no longer get as many dates. In general, the two things that shift a PPF are your resources (here, your time, study skills, and social skills) and your technology—how you combine those resources. The same lessons carry over if the student does three things (say, dating, studying, and sleeping): the PPF becomes three-dimensional, points beyond it are unattainable, points inside it are inefficient, and moving along it means giving up one or more of the other activities.

Shifts of the PPF

Why can’t we produce at a point outside the PPF? Why does the PPF represent the maximum possible output?

  • Limited resources (land, labor, capital, entrepreneurship)
  • Limited technology

Economists group resources into four broad categories: labor (all of our workers—their hours of work and their skills), capital (the actual physical capital, such as an industrial robot in an automobile plant or a rental car—not shares of stock or money a company has saved up), land (all of the natural resources, or “gifts of nature,” including farmland, lumber, and oil), and entrepreneurship (the people who organize businesses and make business decisions—the resource that organizes the other resources). An economy is production inefficient if some of these resources are lying fallow or are misallocated—for example, if people who are good at making machines are making gumballs while people who are good at making gumballs are making machines.

Dr. Rush thinks of technology as a recipe: it tells you how to combine your inputs to get your output. Technology increases when you find new and better ways of producing things you produced before, or when you can produce new things (five years ago we could not produce AI; now we can).

The text defines these two factors as technological change, the development of new goods and better ways of producing goods and services, and capital accumulation, the growth of capital resources, including human capital. If advancements occur in either resources or technology, the PPF can shift outward.

The outward shift that occurs is economic growth, which typically corresponds with an increase in the standard of living. The curve does not have to shift in a parallel fashion. That is, technological advancements could have been much greater for one good than for the other, or vice versa.

Economic growth depends on the decisions regarding what is to be produced. There is an opportunity cost to economic growth. For example, investment in capital and R&D may promote economic growth, but the cost is that the nation must forego consumption in the current period. Dr. Rush illustrates this with gumballs and machines: gumballs stand in for currently consumable goods (restaurant meals, sporting events, or clothing you buy this year), while machines are capital. If the economy produces at a point with more machines and fewer gumballs this year, it has more capital next year, so its PPF shifts out farther and it grows more rapidly—but it had to give up gumballs to do so. Economic growth is not free.

In the United States, the PPF is progressively shifting outward, and our economy typically grows about 1½ to 2% each year because we accumulate more capital, more and better-skilled workers, and better technology. However, in some countries, the PPF has shifted inward. For example, diseases, wars, and natural disasters may cause a country’s PPF to shift inward. The horrific civil war in Syria over the past 20 years destroyed a great deal of capital, and millions of Syrians fled the country, so Syria’s PPF in 2026 is much smaller than it was in 2006. Likewise, the Russian invasion of Ukraine has reduced Ukraine’s production possibilities, so its PPF has shifted inward.

Production Efficiency vs. Allocative Efficiency

We say that every point on the PPF is production-efficient. That is, at these points, we are producing the maximum possible combination of goods by working as efficiently as possible. However, note that, as a practical matter, not every point along the PPF may be equally desirable for all people.

Consider the extremes, where you produce all of one good and none of the other. Clearly, individuals (and societies) prefer variety. So, while those points are production-efficient, they may not be allocatively efficient.

For example, consider a country that produces only food and clothing. Although the point associated with producing only clothing may be production-efficient in that it lies along the PPF, it certainly would not be allocatively efficient. If the country were to produce only clothing, people would be well-clothed, but they would be starving (a nation of incredibly well-dressed corpses, as Dr. Rush put it).

Dr. Rush distinguishes the actual point at which the economy produces from the most desirable point. In Chapter 3, we will use supply and demand to determine the actual point—on which point along a PPF a country actually operates. In Chapter 5, we will use the concepts of marginal social benefit and marginal social cost to determine the most desirable (allocatively efficient) point. We can then compare these two points to determine whether the country is producing in an allocatively efficient manner—a comparison we will return to in Chapters 6, 7, 13, 14, 16, and 17.

The Law of Increasing Opportunity Costs: Why the PPF Is Bowed-Out

Moving along the production possibilities frontier, as more of a good is produced, the opportunity cost of an additional unit increases. Why? Resources are not equally suited for different jobs. For example, some people are better at producing some goods than others.

EXAM TIP: Opportunity Costs Calculation

You will probably need to know how to calculate opportunity costs on the exam. Dr. Rush will never ask you to draw a PPF (the exams are multiple choice), but he may ask for the opportunity cost between two points on a PPF—just take what you lose and divide it by what you gain—and which points are attainable, production efficient, or production inefficient.

The law of increasing opportunity costs is what causes the “bowed-out” shape of the production possibilities frontier. As you move to the right along the PPF, the opportunity cost of the good along the horizontal axis increases and the PPF gets steeper.

EXAM TIP: PPF Shape

PPFs are bowed-out when resources are not equally suited to produce both goods on the PPF.

To calculate the opportunity cost of producing one more gumball, we take the number of machines lost and divide it by the number of gumballs gained. The opportunity costs of moving from left to right along this PPF (from A to E) can be calculated as follows:

  • A to B: 1 machine per gumball
  • B to C: 2 machines per gumball
  • C to D: 3 machines per gumball
  • D to E: 4 machines per gumball

Gumballs, Machines, and the Law of Increasing Opportunity Costs (Cntd.)

The law of increasing opportunity costs states that, as you move along the PPF, when more of one good is produced, the opportunity cost of additional units increases. Why? Resources are not equally suited for different jobs. For instance, some people are better at making gumballs, and some are better at making machines. At first (A→B), workers who are better at making gumballs than machines are reallocated from making machines to making gumballs. But, at the end (D→E), the workers that are being moved from machine production to gumball production are better at making machines than at making gumballs. So, the opportunity cost of producing that last gumball is greater than the opportunity cost of producing the first one. This is why the curve is “bowed-out.” Graphically, each move to the right gains the same one gumball, but the drop down—the machines lost—gets bigger and bigger (1, then 2, then 3, then 4), which produces the bowed-out shape.

If the PPF were a straight line, opportunity cost would not change as you move along the PPF. This would suggest that resources are similarly well-suited to produce both of the goods depicted in the PPF.
