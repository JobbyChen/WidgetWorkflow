What Is Economics?

Economics is the study of the allocation of scarce resources. Economic agents (including individuals, firms, households, and entire societies) face economic decisions because of scarcity, the existence of limited resources alongside unlimited wants.

EXAM TIP: Scarcity

Remember that people must make economic decisions because of scarcity, a situation in which unlimited wants are met with limited resources.

Economists assume that everyone has unlimited wants. In other words, no one can ever be satiated—no matter how much a person has, he or she will still want more. Dr. Knight noted that this assumption is one way in which economics differs from many other social sciences. For example, even the richest person in the world wants to have more money. Individuals will never reach a point where they have no wants.

This is a course in macroeconomics, which studies the “big picture” of the macroeconomy, with a focus on things like gross domestic product, unemployment, and inflation. Before we discuss macroeconomic topics, we have to cover a bit of material from microeconomics, the study of decision making by individuals, households, and firms. We will spend the first third of the course discussing microeconomic topics like supply and demand, and then we will transition into macroeconomic topics for Exam 2 and Exam 3.

Rationality and Decision Making

Economists assume that individuals, firms, and other economic agents are rational and self-interested. This does not necessarily mean that every decision will be the best decision, or even that every decision will ultimately make the economic actor better off. Rather, an economic actor makes rational decisions when it makes the best decision given the information that’s available and the constraints that it faces. For example, if you decided to read these notes, economists would assume that you did so because you believe it is the best possible use of your time. This doesn’t necessarily mean that you enjoy reading notes about economics; rather, you’re reading notes about economics because you think that doing so will lead to a desired outcome—a better grade on your exam.

Rational decision making doesn’t always end in the best outcome, and you don’t have to like a decision for it to be rational. For example, suppose that you buy a new car and decide to wait a month or two before purchasing collision insurance because you have never had an accident and money is tight. If you then get into an accident and total the car, you will regret that decision—but it may still have been the rational choice given the information you had at the time. Likewise, you may be sitting in class even though you would rather be somewhere else. You are there because you believe that the benefits of attending (a better grade, your preferred major, a better job) are worth the cost.

Economic Models

Economists make certain assumptions (such as the assumption that people will act to make themselves as well off as possible), and they use those assumptions to develop economic models—simplified versions of reality—that predict how people will behave. For example, the production possibilities frontier (PPF) and the supply and demand model are two economic models that we will discuss for Exam 1. “Good” economic models are simple enough to observe the relationship between two variables but robust enough to produce results that are broadly applicable in the real world.

Introduction to Opportunity Costs

The opportunity cost of an action or decision is the single highest-valued alternative. That is, it is what you have to give up in order to pursue an alternative activity or get something else. A general definition for the opportunity cost of any decision is as follows:

\[\text{Opportunity Cost} = \frac{\text{What You Lose}}{\text{What You Gain}}\]

Note that the opportunity cost is always expressed in terms of what you lose, or give up. It is the second-best alternative. For example, suppose that you could spend an hour of your time going to class, sleeping, or studying. You rank your options (from most preferred to least preferred) as follows:

EXAM TIP: The next-best alternative

Remember that the opportunity cost of an action is the value of the single, next-best alternative.

  1. Going to class
  2. Playing basketball
  3. Getting lunch

If you decide to go to class, the opportunity cost of spending your hour doing so would be the single next-best alternative—playing basketball. It would not be both playing basketball and getting lunch because you could only do one of these rather than using the hour to go to class.

Opportunity costs do not have to be monetary, and they include both explicit costs (money that you actually pay or give up) and implicit costs (the value of something you forgo without any money changing hands). Economists tend to emphasize implicit costs because they are easy to overlook. For example, Dr. Knight and his husband own a house in Quito, Ecuador, that they choose not to rent out. They never write a check for that decision, but they do give up the rental income they could have earned—an implicit cost. Similarly, many Florida families whose orange groves were wiped out by disease have had to decide whether to keep farming, let the land sit idle, or sell it to developers. A family that keeps its land is giving up the money it could have received by selling, even though that cost never shows up on an accounting statement.

Just as you face opportunity costs and have to make choices involving trade-offs, nations do so, as well. For example, a country that uses its grapes to produce wine cannot use the same grapes to produce grape juice. Therefore, a country has to choose between producing more wine or producing more grape juice. Likewise, a country that decides to have a lot of doctors and teachers will have fewer workers in other sectors.

In the specific context of a nation’s production, the opportunity cost of one additional unit of Good X is defined as follows:

\[\text{Per-Unit Opportunity Cost of Good X} = \frac{\text{# of Units of Good Y Lost}}{\text{# of Units of Good X Gained}}\]

The concept of opportunity costs is extremely important for economists, and professional economics educators rank it as one of the most important concepts in any economics class. Below, we will develop a model that illustrates the concept of opportunity costs and trade-offs with graphs.

The Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)

A production possibilities frontier (PPF) is a graphical representation of the trade-off between two productive activities, such as the production of two goods. That is, it shows the maximum possible combinations of two goods that can be produced with current technology and available resources. A PPF could be drawn for an individual, a household, a firm, or an entire society. We will simplify our analysis by assuming that an economic actor can only produce two goods and that its resources and available technology are both fixed. (The basic ideas that we will extract from the model can be widely applied to a “real” economy with millions of goods, however.) As we will see, this model illustrates the concepts of scarcity, choice, and opportunity cost.

The Basics of the PPF

You can think of the PPF as a frontier between points that are attainable (or feasible) and those that are not attainable. A productively efficient point is one at which society is using all of its available scarce resources. Points inside the PPF are attainable but production-inefficient, points along the PPF are attainable and production-efficient, and points beyond the PPF are simply not attainable. A nation would usually like to produce at a point beyond its PPF, but it is limited by resources and technology.

Points A and B are called points of specialization. At these points, the nation uses all of its productive resources to produce one of the two goods and none of the other. In this case, the firm can use all of its productive resources to produce either 200 units of Good 1 or 400 units of Good 2, but not both. The nation would be better off if it could produce a combination outside the PPF, such as 200 units of Good 1 and 400 units of Good 2. However, this point is simply unattainable. There is not enough time in the day, money in the bank, or workers in the shop to produce more of Good 1 without reducing the production of Good 2.

At points within the PPF, resources are being used inefficiently or are underutilized. For example, workers may be unemployed or factories may be idle at these points. Nevertheless, these points represent possible production points in the sense that the country is capable of producing them. Points along the PPF represent situations in which resources are being fully utilized. These are the most production-efficient points that the country can achieve using current resources and technology.

Again, note that the production possibilities frontier discussed above describes a simple economy in which only two goods are produced. We could, in theory, extend this analysis to multiple goods, but it would make the drawings more complicated.

Note that we will assume that the goods that we consider are infinitely divisible. For example, we can produce 2 Lego sets, 1.9 Lego sets, 1.99 Lego sets, etc. This may not be a reasonable assumption in every case; however, it makes our economic analysis easier.

Note that all of the points along the PPF are productively efficient, meaning that they represent points at which society is using all of its scarce resources as efficiently as possible. Only one of the points along the PPF is allocatively efficient, meaning that it represents the combination of two goods that society values most. For example, if society values boxes of tissues more than Lego sets, the allocatively efficient point may correspond to a lot of tissue production and a small amount of Lego set production.

Dr. Knight will not emphasize the distinction between production efficiency and allocative efficiency in this class, so when a point is described as “efficient,” you can assume that it is “production-efficient.”

An Important Note About PPFs

Note that the production possibilities frontier tells us the maximum combinations of two goods that a society can produce; it does not necessarily tell us the maximum combinations of two goods that a society can consume. As we will see later in this section, a society may be able to consume more than it could possibly produce on its own if it takes advantage of gains from trade.

The PPF and Opportunity Costs

If an economic actor is producing along its PPF, it cannot increase the production of one good without giving up production of another good. In this sense, the PPF illustrates the concept of trade-offs and opportunity costs. The opportunity cost of producing more of one good is the production of the other good that must be given up to do so. That is, the opportunity cost of one additional unit of Good X is calculated as follows:

\[\text{OC}_\text{X} = \frac{\text{# Units Lost of Good Y}}{\text{# Units Gained of Good X}}\]

EXAM TIP: Calculating opportunity cost

Be sure you can calculate opportunity costs between two points along a linear PPF.

Let’s reconsider the PPF that shows the production possibilities for tissues and Lego sets:

We will use this PPF to illustrate several points about opportunity costs:

  • If the PPF is linear—that is, if it is a straight line rather than a curved line—the marginal opportunity costs are constant. For example, moving from Point B to Point C, the country gains 10 Lego sets, but it loses 25 boxes of tissues. The opportunity cost of an additional Lego set, moving from Point B to Point C, is calculated as follows:

    OCLego Sets = \(\frac{\text{25 boxes of tissues}}{\text{10 Lego sets}}\) = 2.5 boxes of tissues per Lego set

    Moving from Point C to Point D, the country gains 10 Lego sets, and it gives up another 25 boxes of tissues. The opportunity cost of a Lego set, moving from Point C to Point D, is calculated as follows:

    OCLego Sets = \(\frac{\text{25 boxes of tissues}}{\text{10 Lego sets}}\) = 2.5 boxes of tissues per Lego set

    As you can see, the opportunity cost of an additional Lego set does not change as more Lego sets are produced. This is because the PPF is linear.

  • Opportunity costs are always expressed in terms of another good. For example, the opportunity cost of producing more Lego sets is the number of boxes of tissues that must be given up to produce one more Lego set, as we move along the PPF.
  • The opportunity cost of one good is simply the reciprocal of the opportunity cost of the other good. For example, let’s calculate the opportunity cost of producing additional boxes of tissues as we move from Point C to Point B:

    OCBoxes of Tissues = \(\frac{\text{10 Lego sets}}{\text{25 boxes of tissues}}\) = 2/5 Lego set per box of tissues

    Note that this is simply the inverse of the opportunity cost of producing more Lego sets.

  • Our simple example uses two specific goods, but we could expand our analysis to describe general categories of goods or multiple goods.
  • If an economic actor is operating within its PPF, it could increase production of one good without giving up production of another good. It could do so, for example, by using its resources more efficiently. For example, if the country were producing 10 Lego sets and 25 boxes of tissues, it could theoretically produce more boxes of tissues without giving up any Lego set production, simply by using its resources more efficiently. Likewise, it could theoretically produce more Lego sets without giving up any tissue production. Therefore, if a country is operating within its PPF, there are no opportunity costs associated with producing more of either good.
  • If an economic actor is operating on its PPF, it cannot increase production of one good without giving up production of another good. For example, if the country is producing 10 Lego sets and 75 boxes of tissues, it cannot produce more boxes of tissues without giving up Lego set production.
  • PPFs have negative slopes because they illustrate situations in which economic actors face trade-offs. Specifically, a nation must give up production of one good to produce more of the other.

Increasing Opportunity Costs and Bowed-Out PPFs

Thus far, we have restricted our analysis to PPFs that are straight lines. When we explore comparative advantage and trade, we will use straight-line PPFs to make our analysis easier because straight-line PPFs have constant opportunity costs. However, in the “real world,” PPFs are almost always not straight lines. Rather, they are concave to the origin. The fact that the slope of a concave PPF changes as you move along the PPF means that the opportunity cost changes as you move along the PPF, as well.

For example, suppose that an economy uses its resources to produce two goods: chai lattes and burritos. Some resources are particularly well-suited for producing chai lattes, while other resources are particularly well-suited for producing burritos.

If the country begins by producing a lot of chai lattes and no burritos, it may be able to increase the production of burritos by taking some of the workers that were making chai lattes and having them produce burritos instead. The rational decision would be to allocate the workers who are best at burrito production (and worst at chai latte production) to the production of burritos. As the economy continues to add more and more burrito production, however, it will have to allocate increasingly good resources for producing chai lattes (and increasingly bad resources for producing burritos) to the production of burritos. As a result, the opportunity cost of producing additional burritos will increase, and the PPF will become steeper to the right.

Let’s calculate the opportunity cost of producing burritos as we move from Point A to Point B in the PPF above:

OCBurritos = \(\frac{\text{10 chai lattes}}{\text{20 burritos}}\) = 0.5 chai latte per burrito

Let’s calculate the opportunity cost of producing burritos as we move from Point B to Point C in the PPF above:

OCBurritos = \(\frac{\text{30 chai lattes}}{\text{20 burritos}}\) = 1.5 chai latte per burrito

As you can see, the opportunity cost of producing burritos increases as more burritos are produced.

EXAM TIP: Bowed-out PPFs

If a PPF is “bowed-out,” opportunity costs are increasing as more of a good is produced.

Concave PPFs reflect the concept of increasing opportunity costs: As a nation increases production of one good, it must give up more and more production of the other good. The PPF is concave to the origin (or “bow-shaped”) when workers have different skills, or when inputs are not equally productive. That is, not all resources are equally suited to produce all goods. In the example above, the same increase in the production of burritos requires larger and larger reductions in the production of chai lattes as we move to the right along the PPF.

The law of increasing opportunity costs also applies as we move from right to left along the PPF. As we do so, society is producing more chai lattes and fewer burritos. Let’s calculate the opportunity cost of producing chai lattes as we move from Point C to Point B in the PPF above:

OCChai Lattes = \(\frac{\text{20 burritos}}{\text{30 chai lattes}}\) = 0.67 burritos per chai latte

Let’s calculate the opportunity cost of producing chai lattes as we move from Point B to Point A in the PPF above:

OCChai Lattes = \(\frac{\text{20 burritos}}{\text{10 chai lattes}}\) = 2 burritos per chai latte

As you can see, the opportunity cost of producing chai lattes increases as more chai lattes are produced.

Dr. Knight used an example from his own kitchen to illustrate why opportunity costs increase. He is very good at grilling steaks but a terrible baker, while his husband is an excellent baker who cannot grill a good steak. Now suppose that an economy has 10 workers—five like Dr. Knight and five like his husband—and that it starts by producing nothing but steaks. If the economy wants to start producing cookies, it should send the best bakers (who are also the worst grillers) into the kitchen first, so very little steak is given up for the first few cookies. As the economy keeps increasing cookie production, however, it eventually has to pull its best grillers off the grill to bake, and it gives up more and more (and better) steaks for each additional cookie. In general, a PPF is bowed-out and opportunity costs are increasing when not all resources are equally suited to producing both goods. A PPF is linear and opportunity costs are constant only when all resources are equally suited to producing both goods. Dr. Knight noted that this is fairly rare in the real world; one possible example would be dividing a fixed amount of study time between two classes, if each additional hour of studying raises your grade by the same amount in either class.

Note that a PPF can never be bowed-in because that would reflect decreasing opportunity costs. This could not occur if resources were rationally allocated. In the steak and cookie example, decreasing opportunity costs would exist if the economy increased cookie production by sending its worst bakers (and best grillers) into the kitchen to make the first cookies. This is not economically rational, so we assume that opportunity costs cannot decrease (and therefore the PPF cannot be bowed-in).

Also, note that because the opportunity cost of one good is the reciprocal of the opportunity cost of the other, there will never be a scenario in which opportunity costs are increasing for one good and constant for the other. That is, opportunity costs are either constant for both goods (and the PPF is a straight line) or they are increasing for both goods (and the PPF is bowed-out). Note that opportunity costs are still reciprocals of one another along a bowed-out PPF, but only between the same two points. For example, the opportunity cost of a burrito moving from Point A to Point B is the reciprocal of the opportunity cost of a chai latte moving from Point B to Point A, but it is not the reciprocal of the opportunity cost of a chai latte moving from Point C to Point B.

Economic Growth and Shifts of the PPF

Economic growth is an increase in the productive capacity of the economy. Note that to an economist, economic growth is an increase in what the economy could produce—not an increase in what it actually produces. This is different from the way the term is used on the news or in the newspapers, where “economic growth” typically refers to an increase in GDP (the amount that the economy actually produces).

We can illustrate economic growth using the production possibilities frontier as an outward shift of the PPF such that a country can produce more of both goods given its resources and technology. That is, economic growth is an expansion of a country’s production possibilities.

The following factors can cause the PPF to shift outward:

  • Increase in the number of available workers – Population growth and immigration (the most common example) increase the size of the labor force, which increases a country’s productive capacity. More workers mean that the economy can produce more total output, though not necessarily more output per person. Note that a reduction in the nation’s unemployment rate is not a factor that shifts the PPF, for reasons that we will discuss below.
  • Technological progress that increases the productivity of productive resources – Technological advances in the production of a good allow the country to produce more of it with the same amount of time and resources. Technological innovation refers to something new, whereas physical capital accumulation (discussed below) simply means that workers have more tools, machinery, and factories available to them.
  • Increase in physical capital – In economics, when we refer to capital, we are referring to physical capital—productive goods like machines and factories. An increase in productive capital allows countries to produce more goods with the same raw materials. (As we will discuss later in the semester, investment specifically refers to an increase in the stock of physical capital, which should make a country more productive.)
  • Increases in human capital – Human capital refers to the skills, knowledge, and experience of humans. An increase in human capital increases productivity and therefore leads to economic growth. Human capital essentially increases the quality of the existing labor force.
  • Increase in the amount of available productive natural resources – While the amount of natural resources on the Earth is fixed, the amount of available natural resources can change due to discoveries of new natural resources.

If the increase in resources or technology affects the production of both goods, economic growth is shown as an outward shift of the entire PPF. If the increase in resources or technology affects the production of just one of the two goods, economic growth is shown as a “pivotal shift” (or rotation) that makes the PPF flatter or steeper.

Below, we have shown each of these possibilities using PPFs for a country that produces two goods: pizza and burritos. Use the buttons to compare them.

When the horizontal intercept shifts outward and the vertical intercept does not change, the opportunity cost of producing burritos decreases and the opportunity cost of producing pizza increases. Likewise, when the vertical intercept shifts outward and the horizontal intercept does not change, the opportunity cost of producing pizza decreases and the opportunity cost of producing burritos increases. (In general, when a PPF gets flatter, the opportunity cost of the good on the vertical axis increases and the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis decreases. When a PPF gets steeper, the opportunity cost of the good on the vertical axis decreases and the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis increases.)

Sector-specific growth (illustrated by a “pivotal” shift) may still allow the country to produce more of the good in the industry that did not benefit from the shift. For example, advances in the production of pizza may allow the country to continue producing the same amount of pizza while devoting some extra resources to the production of burritos.

Above, we showed a parallel shift of the PPF when there was growth in the production possibilities for both goods. When a linear PPF shifts outward in a parallel fashion, its slope does not change, so opportunity costs do not change either. A parallel shift does not have to be the case, however; general productivity growth could affect one good more than the other. When the entire PPF shifts outward (indicating an increase in the ability to produce both goods), the PPF may become flatter or steeper, depending on which good was impacted most. For example, if production of the good listed along the horizontal axis increases by a little and production of the good listed along the vertical axis increases by a lot, the PPF becomes steeper. As a result, the opportunity cost of producing the good on the vertical axis decreases and the opportunity cost of producing the good on the horizontal axis increases. Alternatively, if production of the good listed along the horizontal axis increases by a lot more than production of the good listed along the vertical axis, the PPF becomes flatter, the opportunity cost of producing the good on the horizontal axis decreases, and the opportunity cost of producing the good on the vertical axis increases.

Most nations experience gradual economic growth, so their PPFs shift outward over time. PPFs normally shift outward because economies generally grow over time. However, some economies experience temporary setbacks that cause the PPF to shift inward. Examples include economies that are negatively impacted by wars, diseases, or natural disasters. For example, World War II caused a temporary inward shift of the PPF for most of Europe because it decimated Europe’s physical capital. The explanation that most inward shifts of the PPF are temporary applies best to high-income countries; many low-income countries that experience devastating natural disasters or wars do not recover for quite a while after those events.

The Impact of Reducing Unemployment

Remember that points along the PPF are production-efficient, as they represent situations in which the economy’s resources are fully and efficiently utilized. Unemployment represents a situation in which an economy’s resources are not being used efficiently. When there is unemployment, the country is operating at a point within its PPF. For this reason, reducing unemployment would not cause a shift of the PPF. Rather, it would cause a movement from a point within the PPF to a point closer to the PPF.

The PPF, Comparative Advantage, and the Gains From Trade

A country or an individual has an absolute advantage in producing a particular good or service if it can produce more of that good or service than the other country or individual. Absolute advantage says nothing about quality or efficiency; it simply asks which country could produce more if it devoted all of its resources to that good. For this reason, large countries with more resources usually have absolute advantages over smaller countries. For example, the United States has an absolute advantage over France in producing cheese—not because it is better at making cheese, but because it has more cows, more land, and more workers.

It is not hard to understand why economic units would trade when they each have an absolute advantage in the production of different goods. For example, suppose that you type faster than your roommate and your roommate is faster at cleaning. It would make sense that you would trade your typing service for your roommate’s cleaning services. However, economic theory holds that two economic actors can benefit from trade even if one economic actor has an absolute advantage in the production of all goods. As long as the economic actors have different opportunity costs, they can benefit from specialization and trade.

EXAM TIP: Absolute advantage is not required

Absolute advantage is not necessary for countries to gain from trade. Countries can gain from trade even without an absolute advantage, as long as they have a comparative advantage.

A country or individual has a comparative advantage in producing a particular good or service if it can produce that good or service at a lower opportunity cost than another country or individual. The theory of comparative advantage holds that economic units can gain from trade by specializing in the production of the good for which they have the lowest opportunity cost and trading with one another.

The key difference between absolute advantage and comparative advantage lies in how the cost of production is measured. Absolute advantage considers only the costs of the inputs required to produce the good in question. Comparative advantage, on the other hand, measures costs in terms of the opportunity cost—the amount of the other good that must be foregone to produce one more unit of the good in question. For example, consider a country that produces only bananas and coffee. The opportunity cost of a banana is equal to the number of units of coffee that the nation must give up to produce one more banana.

If we’re considering a simple world in which there are two countries that produce two goods, a single country can have an absolute advantage in the production of none, one, or both of the goods. A country can only have a comparative advantage in the production of one of the goods, however. If a country has a comparative advantage in the production of one good, the other country must have a comparative advantage in the production of the other good.

EXAM TIP: Only one comparative advantage

A country can have a comparative advantage in only one of the two goods it produces.

According to the theory of comparative advantage, a nation or individual should specialize in the production of the good for which it has the lower opportunity cost. As you can see, Mexico has a lower opportunity cost in the production of strawberries (0.67 apples per strawberry vs. 1.5 apples per strawberry) and the United States has a lower opportunity cost in the production of apples (0.67 strawberry per apple vs. 1.5 strawberry per apple). Therefore, Mexico should specialize in producing strawberries, the United States should specialize in producing apples, and they should trade with one another. If they do so at terms of trade that benefit both of them, they will both be able to consume at points outside of their PPFs. (They will continue to produce on their PPFs, however.)

For example, suppose that the United States produces 300 apples, Mexico produces 150 strawberries, and they strike a deal in which they trade 100 apples for 100 strawberries. The final step of the widget above places their consumption points on the PPFs.

As you can see, the United States would be able to consume 100 strawberries and 200 apples—more than it could before. Likewise, Mexico would be able to consume 50 strawberries and 100 apples—more than it could before.

Again, note that specialization and trade don’t allow Mexico or the United States to produce outside their PPFs, but they do allow these nations to consume outside their PPFs.

EXAM TIP: Producing versus consuming

A country can never produce outside of its PPF, but it can consume outside of its PPF with specialization and trade.

Finally, note that the terms of trade (the ratio at which the two goods are exchanged) must fall between the two countries’ opportunity costs, or one of the countries would not voluntarily agree to trade. In the example above, one apple per strawberry lies between each country’s opportunity cost of a strawberry (0.67 apples and 1.5 apples), so both countries benefit. Comparative advantage determines the direction of trade—who specializes in what and which good gets shipped where—but the volume of trade depends on the preferences of the people in each country, which this model tells us nothing about. Dr. Knight may use different countries and goods in class, but the method for identifying comparative advantages is identical.
